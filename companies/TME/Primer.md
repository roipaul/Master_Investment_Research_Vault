# 1. Company Overview

## 1.1 Tencent Music Entertainment Group (TME) — Company Primer

**Source limitations and conventions.** Company figures come from TME's FY2025 Form 20-F, its quarterly earnings releases (6-K exhibits) for Q1 2025 through Q2 2026, the Q3 2025 to Q2 2026 call transcripts and its September 2026 notes filings [1][2][3]. Two primary documents were not retrieved: the HKEX interim report for the six months to June 2026, and Ximalaya's own HKEX prospectus. Three things therefore rest on secondary sources, and are labelled as such where they appear: the Ximalaya consideration as recognised at completion (taken from Music Business Worldwide's reading of the interim accounts), Ximalaya's pre-acquisition financials (from press summaries of its prospectus), and the bank-loan terms. All app-level user data for TME's apps and for ByteDance's Soda Music come from QuestMobile via press or broker reports, because TME stopped publishing user metrics after Q4 2025. TME reports in renminbi (RMB) under IFRS, its fiscal year is the calendar year, and one American Depositary Share (ADS) equals two Class A ordinary shares. TME will report Q3 2026 on November 17, 2026 [4], so Q2 2026 is the latest quarter used here.

### Company Overview

Tencent Music Entertainment Group (TME) runs China's largest group of music apps, QQ Music, Kugou Music and Kuwo Music, together with the karaoke app WeSing, the audiobook app Lazy Audio and, since May 2026, the audio platform Ximalaya [1][2]. Users stream music free or through paid memberships. TME also earns money from advertising, concerts, artist merchandise, digital albums and virtual gifts sent to live-streamers. The company is a Cayman Islands holding company that operates in China through subsidiaries and variable interest entities (VIEs). Its ADSs have traded on the NYSE under "TME" since December 2018, and its Class A shares have traded on the Hong Kong Stock Exchange under "1698" since a secondary listing by introduction in September 2022 [1]. Headquarters are in Shenzhen. Ross Liang (Zhu Liang) has been CEO since April 2021. Cussion Pang, CEO from 2016 to 2021, is Executive Chairman and leads the content ecosystem business. Min Hu has been CFO since 2016 [1]. No succession has been announced.

Scale in 2025: revenue of RMB 32.90bn, up 15.8%; non-IFRS net profit attributable to shareholders of RMB 9.59bn; 127.4m paying music users and 528m online-music monthly active users (MAU) in Q4 2025; more than 20m Super VIP (SVIP) members at year-end; and 5,690 full-time employees, substantially all in China [5][1]. The content library holds more than 300m music and audio tracks [1]. Tencent Holdings controls the company. As of March 31, 2026 it owned 57.2% of the shares and 93.6% of the voting power through high-vote Class B shares. Spotify held 9.0% after a 2017 share swap [1]. Shares issued to Ximalaya's sellers diluted Tencent's beneficial stake, which a June 30, 2026 Schedule 13G/A put at 55.5% of Class A-equivalent shares [6].

The model in one paragraph: TME licenses recordings and compositions from labels, publishers, a collecting society and individual artists, mostly without exclusivity and on one-to-three-year terms. It distributes them through several branded apps aimed at different user groups and makes money four ways. The first is tiered memberships (ad-supported, standard and SVIP). The second is advertising sold to brands. The third is a growing "IP" layer of concerts, artist management, merchandise and digital albums, built on its relationships with artists. The fourth is a shrinking social entertainment business of virtual gifts on karaoke and live-streaming [1]. Tencent's Weixin (WeChat) and QQ are its main sources of user log-ins, traffic and payments [1]. Management describes its strategy as a "content-and-platform" dual engine. From 2026 it has added a goal of becoming a "one-stop music plus audio platform" and an "IP-centric membership" model [5][7][8].

### Segments

TME has **one reportable segment**. Its chief operating decision-maker does not split the business by product, service line or geography for internal reporting, and substantially all revenue and expenses arise in the PRC [1]. It discloses two revenue lines. From Q1 2026 these were renamed and reorganised in a way that breaks historical comparisons.

**2026 reporting change.** From Q1 2026, "online music services" was renamed **"music related services"** with no change to historical amounts. Inside it, the former "music subscriptions" line was replaced by **"membership services"**, defined as fees for membership benefits and privileges, including access to music and audio content and other benefits within music related services. The remainder is labelled **"marketing and consumption services"**, which covers advertising, offline performances, merchandise, digital albums and similar [9][2]. Membership services is a broader measure than the old subscription line. For 2025 it totals RMB 17,907m (quarterly: 4,284 / 4,434 / 4,564 / 4,625), against RMB 17,660m of music subscriptions [9]. TME did not restate earlier years on the new basis [2]. At the same time TME stopped reporting quarterly online-music MAU, paying users and ARPPU, and said it would report total paying users across its music services once a year, as of year-end [5]. The 2026 lines are therefore not like-for-like with the 2023–2025 subscription series. Ximalaya has been consolidated since May 18, 2026 and contributed RMB 407m of Q2 2026 revenue [2].

Each line has different economics. Membership revenue is recurring and prepaid, and it carries the highest margin because content costs are largely shared across the user base. Advertising also adds to margin, though it is exposed to the economy and to competition. Concerts and merchandise have high cost of goods, and management has said they weigh on gross margin as they scale. Social entertainment has been structurally declining since 2021: regulators tightened rules on live-streaming tipping, and TME cut back some interactive features and raised its compliance standards [1][8].

### How the Business Model Works

#### 1. Where revenue comes from, mechanically

**Memberships.** Users of QQ Music, Kugou Music and Kuwo Music can stream basic content without logging in. To buy a package they log in with a Weixin or QQ account or a mobile number [1]. TME sells three tiers. The **ads membership** gives some standard benefits at a lower fee if the user watches a set number of ad videos a day. It was piloted in late 2025 [1][10]. The **standard membership** (on QQ Music, "豪华绿钻" Luxury Green Diamond) offers a fixed number of monthly downloads and access to "pay-for-streaming" songs that non-payers cannot play in full. The **SVIP** tier adds higher-fidelity audio (QQMUSIC AUDIO series, Kugou Viper, DTS, Dolby Atmos and Sony 360 Reality Audio), vocal and instrumental switching, Starlight collectible cards, free streaming of most digital albums, priority concert ticketing, artist-fan events, themed avatars and bundled audiobook and WeSing benefits [1][5][8].

List prices are not in the filings. Third-party price pages show QQ Music standard membership at roughly RMB 15–18 a month or RMB 158–168 a year, and SVIP at RMB 40 a month list, about RMB 28 a month on auto-renewal, or RMB 248–348 a year [11][12]. A cheaper "music package" (音乐包) of about RMB 8 a month also exists. Price pages vary by channel and promotion, so these figures are indicative. The company has said SVIP pays roughly five times the standard RMB 8 package [13]. Revenue from membership packages is recognised mostly over time across the subscription term, and the prepaid portion shows up in deferred revenue: RMB 3.84bn at end-2025 and RMB 4.89bn at June 30, 2026, current and non-current combined [1][5][2]. Users pay through online channels or third-party platforms, including Tencent's. TME records revenue on a gross basis and books channel fees in cost of revenues [1].

From 2026 TME has added **IP-centric memberships** on top of the tiers. Examples are an artist **Fan Club** (the first, with Silence Wang, launched in Q1 2026 with priority ticketing and exclusive content), *bubble* (paid direct messaging with K-pop and Chinese artists, run with DearU) and Weverse DM on QQ Music, run with HYBE [7][1]. After Ximalaya joined, TME began adding long-form audio benefits to SVIP [8].

**Advertising.** Revenue comes from full-screen splash ads when an app opens, banner ads, sponsorships, and the "ad-supported mode", in which users watch ads to unlock songs that would otherwise need payment or to earn rewards [1]. Contracts are priced per display (CPD) or per thousand impressions (CPM) [1]. Group-wide advertising revenue was RMB 3,003m in 2023, RMB 3,978m in 2024 and RMB 4,929m in 2025, or 15% of 2025 revenue [1]. Part of it is sold through Tencent Group, which charges TME advertising fees [1]. Management said advertising, "especially the ad-supported model", faced headwinds in Q2 2026 from the macro environment and competition. It also said Ximalaya added to ad revenue [8].

**IP-related and other music services.** These are offline performances (TME Live, its own concert brands TMEA and TIMA, and full tour production such as G-DRAGON's 20-concert 2025 tour across eight Asia-Pacific cities with more than 260,000 attendees), artist management and brokerage, digital album sales, artist merchandise and collectibles, physical albums, content sublicensing and long-form audio subscriptions [1][5]. Digital albums can be large single events. Jay Chou's *Children of the Sun*, released in March 2026 with SVIP bundles and a campaign across 45 cities, generated more than RMB 100m in sales [9][7]. Concert revenue grew at triple digits year on year in Q1 2026, and IP-related consumption services grew at "strong double-digit" rates in Q2 2026 [7][8].

**Social entertainment.** On WeSing and the live-streaming tabs inside the music apps (plus Kugou Live and Kuwo Live), users buy virtual gifts and send them to performers. TME shares a percentage of that revenue with the performers or their agencies and tries to keep the ratio comparable to peers [1]. Karaoke memberships and ads add a smaller amount. The line fell from RMB 10.4bn in 2023 to RMB 6.2bn in 2025, then to RMB 1.33bn in Q2 2026, down 16.4% YoY [1][2].

The fall in service costs since 2023 has two causes. Revenue-sharing payments dropped as social entertainment revenue shrank and as TME lowered the share paid to performers. Royalties also stopped rising as fast as revenue. TME says content royalty costs "increased year-over-year" in 2025, but total service costs fell 5.2% [1]. Label royalties combine fixed licensing fees, usually paid in instalments over the licence, with revenue-sharing incentive royalties. Minimum royalty commitments on signed contracts stood at RMB 2.59bn at end-2025, RMB 2.29bn of it due within a year [1]. Royalties are mostly fixed or semi-fixed against a growing subscriber base, so each extra subscriber adds more to gross profit than the average subscriber. The SVIP tier, priced higher on the same catalogue, adds further. Management named iOS commission adjustments as a 2026 margin tailwind [10].

The cost that grows fastest is **other cost of revenues**, up 59% in 2025. Concerts, artist brokerage and merchandise carry production and cost-of-goods expenses that streaming does not. Management describes their gross margin effect as close to neutral in quarters when concert efficiency improves, but expects "structural pressure" on gross margin as these businesses grow [7][8]. Below gross profit, operating expenses fell from 18.1% of revenue in 2023 to 14.8% in 2025. That figure includes R&D of RMB 2.3bn, booked within general and administrative expenses, and share-based compensation of RMB 669m [1]. Interest income on the cash pile, RMB 1.05bn in 2025, is booked inside operating profit [1].

#### 3. The demand engine

Membership revenue has three drivers: how many people use the apps (MAU), what share of them pay (paying ratio), and what each payer spends (ARPPU). From 2023 to 2025 all the growth came from the second and third, while the first shrank.

| Online music (annual averages) | 2023 | 2024 | 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|
| MAU (m) | 589 | 570 | 547 |
| Paying users (m) | 100.9 | 117.6 | 125.1 |
| Paying ratio | 17.1% | 20.6% | 22.9% |
| Monthly ARPPU, subscriptions only (RMB) | 10.0 | 10.8 | 11.8 |
| Music subscription revenue (RMB m) | 12,096 | 15,227 | 17,660 |

*Sources: [1]. Figures are not de-duplicated across apps.*

Paying users rose by 24m over two years while MAU fell by 42m. TME attributes ARPPU growth primarily to SVIP expansion and richer membership privileges [14][15]. Quarterly, MAU fell from 556m in Q4 2024 to 528m in Q4 2025 (−5.0%). Paying users rose to 127.4m (+5.3%) and ARPPU to RMB 11.9 (+7.2%) [5]. Membership is recurring. Most revenue comes from auto-renewing monthly and annual plans, and Ximalaya's prospectus, as reported, put retention for its auto-renew and annual members at 75.9% [16]. TME does not disclose retention by tier. It says retention is "very stable" and that SVIP members are growing steadily [7].

The free funnel that feeds paid conversion is now the weak link. In 2026 management attributed the quarter-on-quarter dip in membership revenue in Q1 to competition for free and ad-supported users, mainly on Kugou, whose users are "more price sensitive and promotion sensitive" [7]. In Q2 2026 it said growth in the music business was slowing because of "some impact on traffic", while "high-value users, or SVIP, [have] not been heavily affected" [8]. The cyclical parts of the revenue base are advertising, which depends on brand budgets, concerts and merchandise, which depend on tour calendars and discretionary spending, and social entertainment gifting. Q1 is seasonally light for concerts and Q2 heavy [8].

#### 4. The reinforcing structure, and its post-2021 limits

Management's flywheel works as follows. Scale in users and data attracts labels and artists. Partnerships yield head-start release windows (typically 7–30 days, for example 30 days for P NATION and TF Entertainment releases), co-produced songs and soundtracks. That content drives streaming share and subscriptions, and the artist relationships extend into concerts, merchandise, fan clubs and digital albums, which in turn become SVIP benefits [5][7][8]. Tencent's ecosystem feeds the loop. Weixin and QQ supply log-ins and social sharing, Weixin Video Accounts' song recognition sends viewers to QQ Music, Tencent Video and Tencent Games commission soundtracks (the Honor of Kings 10th-anniversary song drew more than 600m social mentions in two weeks), China Literature licenses novels for audiobooks, and Yuanbao and Weixin's XiaoWei assistant route AI-agent requests to TME content [1][8]. From April 2026 TME runs music operations for Weixin Video Accounts, so a single click can take a user from a short video to playback on QQ Music [7][8].

The limit is legal. In July 2021 SAMR ordered TME to end exclusive music licences within 30 days and to stop paying unreasonably high advances for preferential terms. TME's licences with labels and copyright owners are now non-exclusive [1]. The CEO said in August 2026 that "according to SAMR rules, we cannot use those exclusively on content" when asked about TME's competitive advantage [8]. The flywheel therefore runs through access and relationships: release windows, co-production, artist management and IP-derived products. It cannot rest on owning the catalogue.

#### 5. Go-to-market and transaction model

TME sells directly to consumers through its own apps. Each app targets a different audience. **QQ Music** serves younger users in big cities with a full catalogue and popular artists. **Kugou Music** serves a mass market with strong reach outside the big cities. **Kuwo Music** specialises in in-car listening. Lite sub-brands, **Bodian Music** and **Kugou Concept**, target casual listeners [1]. In Q2 2026 TME launched a **Kugou free version**, and in August 2026 a standalone **"Free Music" (免费音乐)** app on Huawei's AppGallery. In the Free Music app users complete tasks to earn listening time and coins, and recommendations come in a vertical "swipe to listen" feed [8][17][18]. TME also distributes through smart cars (deeper deals with Changan, Li Auto and XPeng in Q2 2026), smart speakers, wearables, PC and an early integration with HarmonyOS [8]. Advertising is sold directly and through Tencent. Concert tickets and merchandise are sold through TME's platforms and partners. In 2026 the company is trimming paid channel spending and leaning harder on Tencent-ecosystem traffic. Selling and marketing expense rose 36% YoY in Q1 2026 "to mitigate the impact of user churn", including user acquisition on ByteDance channels, then rose just 9% in Q2 [7][8][2].

#### 7. Funding and capital model

TME funds itself mainly from operating cash flow, which was RMB 10.2bn in 2025 [1]. Its PRC subsidiaries collect service fees from the VIEs (RMB 20.3bn in 2025), and the VIEs generated 93.6% of 2025 revenue [1]. Revenue is almost entirely in RMB. Moving cash offshore depends on dividends from the PRC subsidiaries, which are subject to PRC withholding tax and reserve rules [1]. Since late 2025 TME has added debt. It drew RMB 3.0bn of RMB bank loans in Q1 2026, and bank borrowings totalled RMB 13.14bn at June 30, 2026, when total debt including notes reached RMB 16.53bn [1][2][19]. In September 2026 it issued US$1.0bn of senior notes, US$500m at 5.050% due 2031 and US$500m at 5.650% due 2036, for general purposes including refinancing offshore debt and share repurchases [3]. It also holds a portfolio of listed music stocks: Spotify shares from the 2017 swap and a 2% stake in UMG received in March 2025. These are carried at fair value through other comprehensive income at RMB 26.2bn at end-2025 and RMB 19.1bn at June 30, 2026 [1][2].

#### AI across TME's value chain

Management talks about AI on every call, and the filings describe it in detail. It touches TME in three places: supply (who makes music and at what cost), consumption (how users find and play music) and governance (what counts as legitimate content).

**Supply-side tools.** TME runs a "one-stop AI music production platform", which management calls Venus. A consumer app called **VEMUS 未音** entered public beta on December 23, 2025, offering songwriting, composition, arrangement, lyrics, cover-accompaniment, beats, rap and remix, driven by chat, text, lyric or image prompts, with a built-in community and charts [7][20]. By end-2025 the platform had served more than 150,000 artists and more than 10m users [5]. An earlier **AI Songwriter** turns a short prompt or custom lyrics into a finished song with vocals that can be published to QQ Music "with one click", using DeepSeek for lyrics [21]. Other tools are AI Tone Magician (a personal AI voice built from a 30-second to 4-minute sample), an eight-stem separation tool, AI-generated vocal demos and track refinement for professional creators, the **Lengjing** AI music-video platform used to fill MV gaps in the catalogue, and the **Lingyin** voice-synthesis engine, which lets users sing in other languages or duet with artists [1][21]. At end-2025 TME opened a dedicated AI-music release channel, the "启明星 AI 实验室", with a RMB 29.9 mastering fee and revenue sharing at an undisclosed rate [22]. Management told investors in May 2026 that AI-generated songs are "a growing portion of new daily releases" and that authorised AI covers of classic hits are among the most popular tracks and send listeners back to the originals [7][23].

**Consumption-side AI.** TME uses a self-developed multimodal model and its Qinwu audio representation model in recommendation, and says the share of streams driven by recommendation hit a record high [5][1]. QQ Music and Kugou carry an AI agent built on Tencent's Yuanbao. It searches for songs, analyses a user's saved library, builds playlists and works as a "personal DJ", and it can complete purchases of digital albums and merchandise from a natural-language request [1][8]. Other consumer features include AI audio-effect matching, one-click vocal extraction, AI Chorus, AI audition and performer, AI-generated lyric cards and backgrounds, Automix song transitions and an AI avatar in the player [1][15]. Management says the agent raises retention, especially among high-value users, but is still testing the Weixin XiaoWei integration. It frames AI's role in monetisation as indirect: more engagement leads to more subscription growth. It added that "Kugou AIK" and AI song generation on QQ Music have "generated very good commercial returns", without giving figures [8].

**Governance.** TME's 2025 ESG report says it removed more than 250,000 policy-violating songs, reviewed more than 600,000 "high-risk copyright" cases, and took down more than 27,000 songs for "song theft" (tampering with ownership data), "song laundering" (plagiarised or altered works) and "trend hijacking". It uses audio fingerprinting, voiceprint and melody comparison to tag AI-generated content, and it rates labels on a "health" scale with penalties for non-compliance [24]. It says it trains AI only on licensed content [24]. The 20-F lists AI as a risk factor, citing unclear ownership and protection of AI-generated works, possible infringement claims, and fast-changing rules (the 2023 Deep Synthesis Provisions, the 2023 AIGC Measures and the January 2026 amendment to the Cybersecurity Law) [1]. Management has also called unauthorised AI content a direct business headwind. The Executive Chairman said in May 2026 that it "creates headwinds for our music subscription growth" [7]. Part 3 covers the competitive side of AI.

### Corporate History

TME was formed in **July 2016**, when Tencent, then a 15.8% holder, took control of China Music Corporation (CMC) by injecting QQ Music and WeSing in exchange for new CMC shares. CMC already held Kugou (launched 2004) and Kuwo (2005). The deal left Tencent with about 61.6% of the merged company, which was renamed Tencent Music Entertainment Group in December 2016 [1]. This merger is the source of TME's multi-app structure and most of its goodwill. SAMR's July 2021 administrative penalty decision concerned this acquisition and required TME to end exclusive music licensing [1].

In **December 2017** TME and Spotify swapped minority stakes. TME issued 282.8m shares to Spotify, and TME and Tencent received Spotify shares. Spotify's co-founder holds the voting rights over the Spotify shares owned by the Tencent group [1]. TME listed on the NYSE in **December 2018**, selling 164m Class A shares as ADSs with selling shareholders [1]. In **2020–2021** it joined a Tencent-led consortium that bought 20% of UMG from Vivendi, with TME holding 10% of the consortium. A distribution in kind in March 2025 left TME holding 2% of UMG directly and produced a RMB 2.37bn deemed-disposal gain [1][5]. In **September 2020** TME issued US$800m of notes, of which the 2025 tranche has been repaid and US$500m of 2.000% notes due 2030 remain outstanding [1].

**2021** brought the largest structural changes. TME bought Shenzhen Lanren (Lazy Audio) for RMB 2.7bn, Ross Liang replaced Cussion Pang as CEO while Pang became Executive Chairman, and SAMR's July order ended exclusive licensing [1]. In **September 2022** TME listed in Hong Kong by introduction, with no new shares [1]. In **June 2025** it agreed to buy Ximalaya for US$1.26bn in cash plus Class A shares equal to up to 5.1986% of its share count, with up to a further 0.37% for the founders [1]. SAMR approved the deal on May 12, 2026 with five behavioural conditions, and it closed on May 18, 2026 [25][2]. The conditions are: no increase in online-audio service prices or cut in service quality, no reduction in the share of free and free-popular content, no exclusive audio licensing (existing exclusives must be ended), no tying of audio or music platforms to automakers, and no restrictions on hosts using multiple platforms [26]. At closing, sellers received up to US$1.26bn in cash and about 175.3m Class A shares [27]. In **March 2026** TME withdrew its quarterly user metrics [5], and in **September 2026** it made its first bond issue since 2020 [3].

## 2.1 Customers and Distribution

### Who Are the Customers

TME's customers are mostly Chinese consumers. Advertisers come second, and corporate buyers of licensed content are a small third group. The company names no customer accounting for 10% or more of revenue. Its customer base is hundreds of millions of individual users, and substantially all revenue is earned in the PRC [1]. The largest identifiable institutional counterparty is Tencent Group. # 3. Industry Overview

## 3.1 Industry Overview

### Introduction

China's online music industry sells access to recorded music and services around it to about 730m internet users who listen to music online, 64.9% of the country's 1.13bn netizens at end-2025 [32]. The core product is on-demand streaming. Around it sit karaoke and music live-streaming (paid for with virtual gifts), long-form audio (audiobooks, podcasts, children's content), and a fast-growing "fan economy" of digital albums, concerts, merchandise and paid artist-fan access. Platforms license recordings and compositions from rights holders, aggregate them into catalogues of tens to hundreds of millions of tracks, and earn money from listeners through subscriptions, advertising and transactions. Rights holders include labels, publishers, the Music Copyright Society of China (MCSC) and a long tail of independent artists.

The industry exists in its current form because of copyright enforcement. Before the mid-2010s, unlicensed downloads were the norm and platforms competed on free access. After regulators moved against unlicensed content, platforms competed on catalogue rights, and for several years they did so through exclusive licences. Tencent's merger with China Music Corporation in 2016 combined the largest catalogue holders and sublicensing operations under one owner [1]. In 2017 the National Copyright Administration began pushing platforms away from exclusive deals. SAMR's July 2021 order then required TME to terminate exclusive music licences and stop paying unreasonably high advances for preferential terms [1]. Since then, content has been non-exclusive and in principle open to any well-funded entrant. Competition has shifted from owning catalogue to distribution, product, pricing and the products built on artist relationships.

The streaming business has a two-sided structure. A platform with more users can pay rights holders more and offers artists more exposure. A platform with more content and better recommendations attracts more users. The structural change of 2024–2026 is that discovery has moved to short video. In China, short video reaches 1.07bn users, 95.4% of netizens [32]. The short-video platform that owns discovery now runs its own music app.

IFPI counts what record companies earn, which is a fraction of what platforms collect from consumers. iResearch counts platform revenue from music streaming and music social entertainment. The university estimate is the broadest and probably includes adjacent digital-music activity. A rough cross-check: the two listed platforms alone reported about RMB 40.7bn of 2025 revenue (TME RMB 32.9bn, NetEase Cloud Music RMB 7.8bn) [5][35]. That is consistent with a platform-revenue market of RMB 45–60bn once ByteDance's music apps, Ximalaya and smaller players are added, with the RMB 115bn figure covering a wider perimeter. TME gives no TAM figure of its own. It describes itself as the largest online music entertainment platform in China by MAU [1].

CLSA's 2024 initiation reported paid penetration in mature markets of 53–61% (US, Germany, Sweden) and judged China's long-run potential lower, at about 40%, because Chinese listeners use live-streams and short video more and 78% of surveyed Chinese users reported using unlicensed sources, against 30% globally (IFPI survey data) [30]. Globally, IFPI counted 837m paid streaming accounts in 2025, and paid subscriptions made up 52.4% of recorded-music revenue [34].

### How the Industry Works

**Value chain.** Creators (songwriters, performers, producers) sign with labels and publishers, or self-release through distributors and platform creator programmes. Rights holders license two separate rights, the sound recording and the underlying composition and lyrics, to digital service providers (DSPs). DSPs aggregate catalogue, build discovery products and collect from consumers and advertisers. They pay rights holders through fixed fees, minimum guarantees, revenue shares or per-stream rates. They pay live-streamers and karaoke performers a share of gift revenue. In China the composition side is partly licensed through MCSC, which TME signed new agreements with in 2025, paying an advance plus a percentage of revenue [1]. Supply is fragmented: in 2024 CLSA, citing CIC, put the three international majors at about a third of mainland sales, and the top-10 recording companies' share fell from 40% in 2019 to 35% in 2022 [30].

**Business models.** Four models sit side by side, often inside the same app.

| Model | Who pays | Pricing unit | Margin character | Example |
|---------|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| Paid subscription (tiered) | Listener | Monthly/annual fee; RMB ~8 to 40 per month across tiers | Highest once content costs are covered; recurring | QQ Music, Kugou, NetEase Cloud Music |
| Free, ad-funded | Advertiser (listener pays in time/attention) | CPM/CPD; users watch ads to unlock listening | Lower revenue per hour; cheap acquisition when traffic comes from an affiliated platform | Soda Music, Tomato Music, TME's Free Music app |
| Social entertainment | Tipping fan | Virtual gifts; platform keeps a share after paying performers | Revenue-share cost; regulated | WeSing, Kugou Live |
| IP / fan economy | Superfan | Album copy, ticket, merchandise, fan-club fee | Lower gross margin; lumpy; relationship-intensive | Digital albums, concerts, *bubble* |

*Sources: [1][36][17]*

**Pricing.** Paid tiers have converged. QQ Music, Kugou and NetEase Cloud Music charge similar standard prices, roughly RMB 15–18 a month or about RMB 158 a year. Soda Music's VIP is around RMB 8 a month or RMB 88 a year [36][37]. Platforms widen the range with super-premium tiers (TME's SVIP at about RMB 40 a month list) and with free tiers paid for in ad viewing. On Soda, about 80 seconds of ads unlocks a day of member benefits, and the time can be stacked [36][11]. Joint memberships with video, e-commerce and travel platforms are common promotional channels [11].

**Platform and ecosystem effects.** Distribution is tied to China's two biggest attention ecosystems. TME draws traffic, log-ins and payments from Tencent's Weixin and QQ [1]. ByteDance's Soda Music is built as the full-track destination for songs heard on Douyin. QuestMobile reported that 82.1% of Soda's users came from Douyin in June 2025 [38], and Soda syncs Douyin favourites into its library [39]. In 2024, as Douyin's music licences came up for renewal, ByteDance reportedly asked rights holders to license the same content to Soda as well, which quickly built Soda's catalogue [39]. ByteDance apps reportedly took about 40% of time spent across China's top-50 apps in June 2026 [17].

### Demand Drivers and Cyclicality

Demand for listening is steady, but how it is monetised is not. Time spent on music depends little on the economy. What varies is the paying ratio (driven by paywalls, catalogue, product quality and cheaper alternatives), ARPPU (tiering and discounts) and the discretionary layers: advertising budgets, concert attendance, merchandise and gifting. Social entertainment has proved the most fragile. Its revenue depends on regulation and on competition from general live-streaming on short-video apps. Seasonality is mild for subscriptions and strong for concerts. TME's concerts are weighted toward Q2 and Q4, and Q1 is seasonally light [7][8].

**The recent cycle.**

### Key Industry Trends and Developments

**1. Free, short-video-led streaming returns, on a new model.** ByteDance's Soda Music (汽水音乐, launched June 2022) went from about 30m MAU in early 2024 to 120.4m in September 2025 (+90.7% YoY), 156.1m in March 2026 (+78%) and 167m in June 2026 (+68.7%), passing NetEase Cloud Music to become the third-largest music app [30][44][45][18]. A second ByteDance app, Tomato Music (番茄畅听音乐版), aims at lower-tier and older users with "listen and earn" incentives. Together the two had 211m mobile MAU in Q2 2026, up 67% YoY [41]. The model has free access as default, ads that unlock member time, VIP at roughly half the incumbents' price, and acquisition through Douyin at almost no cost [36][38]. Soda said in March 2026 that its user base grew 120% in 2025 and that 25% of its users were new to music streaming [46]. Growth is slowing. Soda and Tomato added 8m MAU quarter on quarter in Q2 2026, against 23–30m in each of the three prior quarters. Soda's iOS grossing nonetheless kept growing more than 200% YoY, which CLSA reads as rising paid conversion from a small base [41]. Incumbents have answered with their own free entry points, including TME's Kugou free version and Free Music app [8][17].

**2. AI-generated music as a supply shock.** Generative tools have made song creation nearly free. On the supply side, ByteDance's Seed team released the Seed-Music models in September 2024 (score-to-song, lyric editing, voice cloning) and added music generation to its Doubao assistant [47]. TME offers VEMUS / Venus and AI Songwriter, and NetEase Cloud Music launched AI songwriting and singing tools [20][21][35]. Platforms pay for AI content in different ways. Press reporting in April 2026 cited industry sources putting AI music on TME at RMB 0.2–0.4 per 1,000 plays (against RMB 1–2 for human originals). Soda paid about RMB 0.8–1.5 per 1,000 effective plays plus Douyin background-music rewards, and Douyin runs an AI music creation lab whose output Soda and Tomato distribute. NetEase pays play incentives, ran a RMB 1m AI songwriting contest and is the only one that commits to AI works belonging to their creators [22]. In March 2026 TME's CEO said AI songs had begun to appear on charts in the previous three months, mostly AI re-sung versions of existing songs boosted by short video [10]. In May 2026 the Executive Chairman said unauthorised AI content "creates headwinds for our music subscription growth". The CEO accused unnamed competitors of using AI "to quickly fill their music libraries" and divert light users, calling it "an exhaustion of the economic value of this industry" [7][43]. Abroad, Deezer reported about 75,000 fully AI-generated uploads a day (about 44% of uploads) in April 2026. Spotify has removed more than 75m "spammy" tracks and introduced AI credits and a verification badge [24]. The majors have moved from litigation to licensing: UMG settled with Udio in October 2025 and WMG with Suno in November 2025, each agreeing licensed AI platforms [48]. UMG's digital chief argues that AI content volume "doesn't have a market", and Believe puts AI at under 0.5% of streams [43]. In China, the Sept 2025 labelling measures are the main tool for platforms to separate AI from human content at upload [42].

**3. "Song washing" and catalogue integrity.** Cheap production has fed grey-market practices: tampering with ownership data ("song theft"), lightly altered copies of existing works ("song laundering") and opportunistic uploads riding trends. TME removed more than 27,000 such tracks in 2025 and more than 250,000 policy-violating songs in total, and it co-founded the Copyright Society of China's AI copyright working committee [24]. Management said in May 2026 that it would "do everything in our power to suppress song-washing" [7]. How well platforms police catalogue quality has become a way for them to set themselves apart, and for rights holders, a reason to choose one platform over another.

**4. Monetisation shifts from access to fandom.** With catalogue no longer exclusive, incumbents are building products that sell scarce access to artists rather than access to songs. These include tiered super-premium memberships, digital albums with head-start windows, artist messaging (*bubble*, Weverse DM), fan clubs, concert production and merchandise [1][7]. TME's non-subscription online-music revenue rose 39.2% in 2025 to RMB 9.07bn [5]. NetEase Cloud Music grew membership while cutting social entertainment [35]. Soda's offline activity runs at a different scale: 36 shows and festivals in 16 cities drawing 500,000 attendees in 2025 [46].

**5. Long-form audio consolidates under pressure from free models.** Ximalaya, the largest independent audio platform, grew revenue only 1.7% in 2023, to RMB 6.16bn [16]. ByteDance's free audiobook app Fanqie Changting (番茄畅听) passed 100m MAU [49]. TME's acquisition of Ximalaya puts roughly half of China's online audio market under one owner (a combined 45–55% share by SAMR's measure, as reported) [27], subject to SAMR conditions that freeze pricing and free-content ratios and ban exclusivity [26][25]. Ximalaya's MAU fell 21.8% YoY in June 2026, according to QuestMobile [18].

**6. Ecosystem traffic over paid acquisition.** Each side now routes its own short-video traffic into music. ByteDance does this from Douyin to Soda, and since April 2026 Tencent does it from Weixin Video Accounts to QQ Music. AI assistants are becoming a new entry point: Tencent's Yuanbao and XiaoWei for TME, ByteDance's Doubao on the other side [7][8][47].

**7. Listening beyond the phone.** In-car, smart-speaker, wearable and OS-level integrations are growing. Kuwo is positioned for cars, and SAMR's Ximalaya conditions specifically bar tying audio or music platforms to automakers [1][26].

## 3.2 Industry Competitive Dynamics

China's online music market has three groups of players: Tencent (TME), ByteDance (Soda Music, Tomato Music, and Douyin as the discovery layer), and NetEase (NetEase Cloud Music). Long-form audio is a neighbouring market, now led by TME through Ximalaya and Lazy Audio, with ByteDance's Fanqie Changting as the main challenger. Alibaba (Xiami) and Baidu left music streaming earlier, after failing to secure copyrights [30].

### Soda Music in Detail

**How it was built.** ByteDance first tried a domestic streaming service in 2019 and dropped it, partly because TME then held exclusive rights to major-label catalogues [39]. After SAMR's 2021 order, ByteDance could license on equal terms, and Soda launched in June 2022 [39]. CLSA's February 2024 initiation put Soda at about 30m MAU, about 4% of the online-music user base. CLSA judged it "not considered by most online music subscribers as a popular substitute" because its catalogue was limited, and expected little near-term threat [30]. The 2024 licence-bundling strategy and Douyin's traffic changed that within 18 months [39][44].

**Its product.** Soda's design copies Douyin: a vertical, algorithm-driven feed that plays immediately and recommends by mood or genre, with an option to search [39]. Many viral versions of songs (DJ remixes, covers, sped-up or pitch-shifted edits) exist only on Douyin and Soda, which gives Soda a catalogue of its own even with fewer licensed tracks [53].

**Who uses it and where the users came from.** QuestMobile counted users who split their time between Soda and Kugou at 21.49m in September 2025 (+63.9% YoY) and between Soda and QQ Music at 21.58m (+72.9%) [38]. Soda's users skew male, born in the 1980s and 1990s, and live in lower-tier cities. That overlaps most with Kugou's mass-market base, and Kugou's MAU fell fastest [37][18]. TME management's account matches this: competitive pressure is concentrated on Kugou's price-sensitive, free and ad-supported users, while QQ Music's operating data are "steady and healthy" and SVIP is "not heavily affected" [7][8].

**How it makes money and what it pays.** Soda's model is built for scale first. Members get ad-free listening and lossless audio at prices well below the incumbents', and non-members unlock member time by watching ads [36]. Its creator payouts for AI and independent music, as reported, are higher per play than TME's, and Douyin background-music rewards come on top [22]. For labels, the 2024 bundling meant Douyin licences began producing streaming royalties from Soda, which earlier had mostly gone only to super-IP holders [39]. Soda's iOS grossing grew more than 200% YoY in mid-2026 [41]. Revenue, paying users and margins are not disclosed.

**What it lacks.** Soda reportedly has about 50m tracks against TME's more than 300m. Reports say it is weaker on classic and back-catalogue albums. It has no material concert, artist-management or merchandise business at TME's scale, and it offers no equivalent of TME's SVIP fan benefits [39][1][46].

### Level of Competitive Intensity

Intensity is **high and rising** overall, and it varies a great deal by tier.

**Free and casual listening: very high.** This is where Soda and Tomato compete directly. ByteDance's cost of acquiring users through Douyin is close to zero, its pricing is about half the incumbents', and it pays creators competitively. Incumbents have cut prices (Kugou Concept's "more flexible pricing"), launched free apps and raised acquisition spend. TME's Q1 2026 selling and marketing expense rose 36% before being reined in [7][8]. TME's MAU fell 5% in 2025, and Kugou, Kuwo and WeSing lost 10–30% of MAU YoY by June 2026 [5][18].

**Standard paid tier: moderate and rising.** Subscription growth at both incumbents slowed sharply in 2026. TME's membership revenue was flat quarter on quarter through 1H 2026 once Ximalaya is excluded (derived), and NetEase's membership grew 4% in H1 2026 [9][2][40]. Management attributes the pressure to weaker new-member growth on Kugou and to "disorderly price competition" [7].

**SVIP and fan products: lower.** The products here depend on artist relationships, concert production capacity and K-pop and Chinese idol partnerships that new entrants cannot license off the shelf. TME says SVIP keeps growing, and IP-related services grew at double digits in Q2 2026 [8]. Soda's offline business exists but is small [46].

**Social entertainment: high.** Live-streaming competes with every short-video and live-commerce platform, under tight regulation. TME's social entertainment revenue fell 16.4% YoY in Q2 2026, and NetEase's fell 32% in 2025 [2][35].

**Long-form audio: rising.** ByteDance's free Fanqie Changting competes with Ximalaya's paid model, and SAMR's conditions stop TME from raising audio prices or narrowing free content [49][26].

**Barriers.** Barriers to entry fell when exclusivity ended, because any well-funded platform can now license the same catalogue. Barriers to scale remain. They include hundreds of label negotiations, MCSC coverage, recommendation data, artist relationships, concert operations and, above all, a traffic source. Only platforms with their own short-video or social ecosystem have entered successfully since 2021 [30][39].

### Basis of Competition

| Dimension | What it means in Chinese online music | Who it favours |
|---|---|---|
| Traffic / discovery source | Where users first hear songs (short video, social feeds) and how cheaply they can be converted to listening | ByteDance (Douyin → Soda); Tencent (Weixin, Video Accounts → QQ Music) |
| Catalogue breadth and depth | Non-exclusive licences for hits plus back catalogue, classics, K-pop and J-pop | TME (>300m tracks, long label relationships); gap narrowing |
| Price and free access | Headline price, discounting, ad-unlocked free tiers | ByteDance; incumbents responding with lite and free apps |
| Exclusive windows and premium content | 7–30-day head starts, digital albums, co-produced hits, soundtracks | TME (label partnerships, Tencent Video/Games IP) |
| Fan and IP services | Concerts, artist management, merchandise, fan clubs, artist messaging | TME (scale and production capability), NetEase (indie community) |
| Recommendation and product | Feed-style discovery, personalisation, AI agents, audio quality | Contested: ByteDance strong on feeds; TME on audio tech and AI agents |
| Creator economics | Payouts per play, promotion support, AI-music terms | ByteDance higher per-play payouts as reported; NetEase on AI ownership |
| Catalogue integrity and copyright enforcement | Policing AI, song washing and infringement; rights-holder trust | Incumbents with enforcement tools (TME) |
| Regulatory standing | Compliance with antitrust conditions and content rules | Smaller players face fewer conduct constraints; TME bound by SAMR orders |

*Sources: [1][30][22][24][7][8][26]*

### Structural Dynamics and Outlook

Three dynamics stand out. First, the end of exclusivity and the arrival of a free, ad-funded entrant with its own traffic have split the market into a mass free tier, where scale and acquisition cost decide outcomes and ByteDance holds the advantage, and a paid tier for engaged listeners and fans, where catalogue depth, artist relationships and IP services count most and TME remains the largest player. Second, AI is cutting the cost of new songs toward zero. That favours platforms that pay per play and promote by algorithm. It also makes curated, licensed and human-made premium content scarcer, and it raises the value of strong catalogue governance. Rights-holder strategy abroad (licensing AI platforms rather than banning them) suggests Chinese labels may also try to monetise AI rather than block it, though China has no clear legal framework for AI-music ownership [48][43]. Third, regulation constrains the leader more than the challengers. TME is bound by the 2021 exclusivity order and the 2026 Ximalaya conditions, while ByteDance is not under equivalent orders in music.

Contested ground in 2026–2027 includes the casual listeners on Kugou and Kuwo, conversion of Soda's large free base to paying members, ownership of AI-music supply and its economics, and long-form audio, where free and paid models now compete directly. **Summary:** China's music streaming market has moved from a stable TME–NetEase duopoly to a three-way contest in which ByteDance leads user growth through Douyin-funnelled free listening. Incumbents keep the bulk of paid revenue and are moving their value toward fandom and IP. AI-generated content adds supply faster than demand, which makes quality control and premium IP more important.

Additional paid-in capital rose RMB 5.0bn, mainly from the shares issued to Ximalaya's sellers. Treasury shares rose RMB 2.7bn from the Q2 buyback. Bank borrowings went from zero to RMB 13.1bn [5][2]. Total consideration recognised for Ximalaya was about RMB 14.0bn, of which about RMB 8.6bn was cash, as reported from the interim accounts [19]. Net cash fell from RMB 34.5bn to RMB 27.7bn (derived), and the US$1.0bn notes issued in September 2026 added gross debt after the period end [3]. Listed equity stakes fell from RMB 26.2bn to RMB 19.1bn. The release does not explain the change, and the matching fall in other reserves (RMB 22.5bn to RMB 16.5bn) suggests it went through other comprehensive income [2].

*Organic growth has slowed sharply and the mix is shifting toward lower-margin revenue.* In Q2 2026 organic revenue grew about 1% and organic music-related revenue about 5%. Organic membership appears roughly flat sequentially (estimate). Marketing and consumption services, the faster-growing line, have higher cost of revenues. "Other cost of revenues", which includes concert production, ad commissions, channel fees and merchandise, rose 59% in 2025 to 38% of COGS [1][2]. Management guides to slightly lower gross and net margins in 2H 2026 [8].

### Key risks to the outlook

**Erosion of the free-to-paid funnel.** TME's paid growth depended on converting free users. QuestMobile data show Kugou down 10.7% YoY, Kuwo down 18.5% and WeSing down 29.7% in June 2026, while Soda rose 68.7% to 167m MAU [18]. Organic membership revenue has been roughly flat since Q4 2025 (derived). Management's response, free apps and more acquisition spend, costs money and may cannibalise existing tiers [9][2][7].

**AI-generated content and catalogue economics.** Management calls unauthorised AI content a headwind to subscription growth and says competitors are using AI "to quickly fill their music libraries" [7]. Payouts for AI music differ across platforms, and China has no clear rule on who owns AI-generated works [22][43]. The 20-F flags infringement claims, ownership uncertainty and fast-changing AI regulation as risks [1].

**Ximalaya integration under SAMR conditions.** Ximalaya added RMB 407m of revenue in about six weeks, while its MAU fell 21.8% YoY in June 2026 [2][18]. SAMR's conditions bar price increases, cuts to free content and exclusive deals [26]. Purchase accounting added RMB 9.2bn of goodwill and RMB 3.0bn of intangibles, and management lowered its 2H 2026 margin outlook partly because of the consolidation [2][8].

**Mix shift and margin.** IP-related revenue grows fastest but has higher costs and is seasonal and lumpy. A single album such as Jay Chou's, or a single tour, can swing a quarter. Management expects structural gross-margin pressure as this business grows [7][9].

**Reduced disclosure.** From 2026 TME publishes no quarterly MAU, paying users or ARPPU. The next hard read on the paid base is the year-end 2026 paying-user count. Until then, third-party app data and the membership revenue line are the only proxies [5].

**Social entertainment and advertising.** Social entertainment fell 16.4% YoY in Q2 2026 and remains exposed to content and tipping regulation. Advertising, especially the ad-supported model, faced macro and competitive headwinds in Q2 [2][8][1].
