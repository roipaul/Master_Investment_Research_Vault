## 1.1 Yunnan Aluminium (000807.SZ) — Company Primer

### Company Overview

Yunnan Aluminium (云南铝业股份有限公司, "云铝股份") is an integrated aluminium producer based in Kunming, in China's south-western province of Yunnan. Its A-shares trade on the Shenzhen Stock Exchange under the code 000807 [1]. It mines bauxite, refines alumina, smelts primary aluminium, makes the carbon anodes and cathodes used in smelting, and casts much of its metal into alloy ingots and rod [1]. In 2025 the company had revenue of RMB 60.04bn, net profit attributable to shareholders of RMB 6.05bn, operating cash flow of RMB 8.43bn and a weighted-average return on equity of 19.7% [1].

The company's capacities are as follows [1]:

- alumina: 1.40 Mt a year;
- electrolytic (primary) aluminium: 3.08 Mt;
- anode carbon: 0.80 Mt;
- graphitised cathodes: 20 kt;
- "green" aluminium alloy: 1.61 Mt.

That smelting capacity makes it one of the larger primary aluminium producers in China. National capacity is capped at 45 Mt a year, so Yunnan Aluminium's 3.08 Mt is roughly 7% of the capped total (inference from [1] and [19]). The defining feature of the company is its power source. Its smelters draw on Yunnan's grid, which is dominated by hydropower. The company says clean energy supplied over 80% of its production power in 2024 [2], and secondary sources put the share at about 87.5% in 2025 [4]. It describes its metal as "green aluminium" with roughly a fifth of the carbon emissions of coal-powered aluminium [2].

The company is a state-owned enterprise in the Chinalco system. Aluminum Corporation of China Ltd (Chalco, itself listed in Shanghai and Hong Kong) holds 29.10%. Yunnan Metallurgical Group holds 13.00%. Both are controlled by Aluminum Corporation of China (Chinalco Group), so the group controls 42.1% in total [1][3]. Chalco is also the company's largest customer and largest supplier (Part 2) [2]. That makes the Chinalco relationship the single most important structural fact about the business after power.

The year to date has been exceptional. In the first nine months of 2026 revenue was RMB 52.62bn, up 19.4%. Attributable net profit was RMB 11.61bn, up 164%, so nine months of 2026 earned almost twice the whole of 2025 (secondary [9][10]). The gross margin for the nine months was 32.0%, against 16.8% for 2025 and 13.2% for 2024 [2][5][10]. The driver was a wide spread between a high aluminium price (about RMB 23,700–24,200/t in 2026) and a low alumina price (about RMB 2,700/t) (secondary [6][8][9]). Part 4 sets out the quarterly path.

### Segments

The company does not report operating segments in the IFRS 8 sense. The annual report gives revenue and gross margin by product line. In 2024 it split revenue into electrolytic aluminium, aluminium processing (alloy and rod) and "other" [2].

| Product line (2024) | Revenue (RMB bn) | % of revenue | Gross margin |
|---|---|---|---|
| Electrolytic aluminium | 30.97 | 56.9% | 13.24% |
| Aluminium processing (alloys, rod) | 22.84 | 41.9% | 13.42% |
| Other | 0.63 | 1.2% | 6.04% |
| Total | 54.45 | 100% | 13.23% |

*Source: [2].*

Two points follow from the table. First, alumina and carbon products are not separate revenue lines. Almost all of the company's alumina and anodes are consumed internally by its own smelters, so they show up as lower costs, not as revenue [2]. Second, "aluminium processing" is mainly primary metal cast into alloy ingot or electrical rod at the smelter, often shipped as molten metal or near-primary product. It is not a separate fabrication business with its own economics. That is why its margin tracks the smelting margin almost exactly: 13.42% against 13.24% in 2024. Broker notes reproducing the 2025 annual report put 2025 gross margins at 18.2% for electrolytic aluminium and 15.2% for processing [4]. For H1 2026 they put them at 34.04% and 29.87% [8]. The gap between the two lines has widened slightly in the high-price period. A likely reason, not stated by the company, is that alloy products carry a conversion fee that does not rise with the metal price.

Operationally the business is run through a set of plant subsidiaries, each built around a smelter. The 2024 annual report names the following [2]:

- **Yunnan Wenshan Aluminium (文山铝业)**, Wenshan prefecture: bauxite mining, the company's only alumina refinery, and smelting. 2024 net profit RMB 1.60bn.
- **Yunnan Haixin Aluminium (海鑫铝业)**, Zhaoyang, Zhaotong. 2024 net profit RMB 1.12bn.
- **Yunnan Zexin Aluminium (泽鑫铝业)**, Fuyuan.
- **Yunnan Yongxin Aluminium (涌鑫铝业)**, Jianshui.
- **Yunnan Runxin Aluminium (润鑫铝业)**, Gejiu.
- **Qujing Shenxin (淯鑫)**, Zhanyi.
- **Heqing Yixin (溢鑫铝业)**, Heqing.
- **Yunnan Yuanxin Carbon (源鑫炭素)**, Jianshui: anode production.
- **The parent's Yangzonghai plant**, near Kunming.

Several of these subsidiaries were set up with partners, so minority interests have been material. Buying them in has been a recurring use of cash (see Corporate History and Part 4).

### How the Business Model Works

**The unit of account is a tonne of aluminium.** Yunnan Aluminium sells a commodity at a price it does not set. The domestic price is anchored on the Shanghai Futures Exchange (SHFE) and the Changjiang (Yangtze) spot market. Profit is therefore the spread between that price and the cost of making a tonne. The cost has four main parts:

- **Alumina.** About 1.9–2.0 tonnes of alumina per tonne of aluminium.
- **Electricity.** About 13,000–13,500 kWh per tonne in a modern Chinese smelter.
- **Carbon anodes.** About 0.45–0.5 tonnes per tonne.
- **Labour, maintenance, depreciation and other materials.**

These ratios are industry engineering norms, not company disclosures. They are given only to show how the cost stack works.

| Illustrative cost element per tonne of aluminium | Physical input | Indicative 2026 price | Indicative cost (RMB/t) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alumina | ~1.93 t | ~RMB 2,700/t [9] | ~5,200 |
| Electricity | ~13,500 kWh | ~RMB 0.43/kWh incl. tax (broker assumption) [11] | ~5,800 |
| Carbon anode | ~0.48 t | ~RMB 6,000/t (broker assumption) [11] | ~2,900 |
| Other cash costs and depreciation | — | — | ~2,000–2,500 |
| Total, before VAT adjustments | | | ~15,900–16,400 |

*Illustrative only. The physical ratios are industry norms. The prices are from broker notes [9][11], not company disclosure. The company does not publish a unit cost.*

Against an aluminium price of roughly RMB 23,700/t including 13% VAT, about RMB 21,000/t before VAT, this sketch implies a cash spread in the region of RMB 5,000/t or more. That is consistent with the reported gross margins of 31–32% in 2026 (secondary [8][10]). The spread is the whole story of the company's earnings. A RMB 1,000/t move in the aluminium price, applied to the 3.19 Mt of aluminium products targeted for 2026 [4], changes pre-tax gross profit by roughly RMB 2.8bn a year after VAT. A RMB 100/t move in alumina changes it by roughly RMB 0.6bn (inference; the company gives no sensitivity).

**Power is the strategic asset, and it is borrowed.** Yunnan Aluminium does not own most of the hydropower that runs its smelters. It buys electricity from the provincial grid, partly through annual and medium-term contracts traded on Yunnan's power market [2]. The province's cheap, low-carbon hydropower is why the company is there, and why China Hongqiao and Shenhuo have moved capacity into Yunnan as well (Part 3) [27][29]. Hydropower is seasonal, though. In the dry season, roughly November to May, the province has repeatedly ordered smelters to cut load:

- **September 2022 to early 2023:** three rounds of cuts, of about 10%, 20% and 40% [35].
- **Early 2023:** Reuters reported that Yunnan Aluminium, along with Chalco and Shenhuo, had cut Yunnan output by more than 40% [32].
- **Winter 2023–24:** a further dry-season curtailment of nearly 400 kt across the province [34].

The 2024 annual report lists hydropower variability and tariff changes among the company's main risks [2]. The business model therefore pairs one of the lowest-carbon cost positions in China with an availability risk that coal-powered rivals in Xinjiang and Inner Mongolia do not carry. In August 2026 the board added power supply, generation and transmission to the company's business scope [3], which may signal an intention to secure some of its own power. Its Yunnan neighbour China Hongqiao is building solar specifically to steady dry-season supply [26]. The company has not said what it plans.

**The company is structurally short alumina.** It can refine 1.40 Mt of alumina a year and produced 1.304 Mt in 2025 (secondary [4]). Its smelters need roughly 1.93 t per tonne of aluminium. At about 3.2 Mt of aluminium output, that is about 6 Mt of alumina, so the company buys around three-quarters of what it uses (inference). The 2024 annual report shows the largest supplier was Chalco, at RMB 23.54bn or 50.27% of purchases [2]. The natural reading is that Chalco supplies most of the purchased alumina; the report does not itemise the purchases. This short position is why falling alumina prices since 2025 have been so good for margins. Q1 2026 cost of sales fell 13.6% year on year while revenue rose 13.7% (secondary [6]).

**Upstream integration is partial and slow to change.** The company holds bauxite resources around Wenshan and runs a mine-to-refinery chain there [2]. It has bid for new exploration rights, including at Taiyangba in Zhaotong, and has tried to convert exploration rights into mining rights at Wenshan and Heqing (secondary [4][12]). The 2025 annual report says the Wenshan bauxite project was delayed because mining rights for the Yangliujing and Dashipen deposits had not been granted, held up by forest-land, land-use and highway issues [1]. This came only months after a provincial review had approved the Yangliujing mining plan, at a planned 600 kt a year (secondary [12]). Bauxite self-sufficiency is not quantified [2].

**What is recurring and what is cyclical.** Volume is close to fixed. Capacity is capped nationally, the smelters run at or near full load when power allows, and the 2026 production target (3.19 Mt of aluminium products) is slightly below 2025 output (3.226 Mt) (secondary [4]). Almost all of the earnings variance therefore comes from two commodity prices and one policy-dependent input, power. The company's own levers are smaller:

- unit power consumption;
- anode self-supply;
- buying out minority interests in its smelters, which raised attributable smelting capacity by more than 150 kt in 2025 (secondary [6]);
- extending into alloy, recycled aluminium and aluminium-ash recovery [1][16].

**How the pieces reinforce each other.** Three features compound:

1. **Cost.** Cheap hydropower, plus parent-supplied alumina, puts the company low on the domestic cost curve when water is plentiful.
2. **Finance.** The parent's finance arms (Chinalco Finance, a factoring company and a leasing company) give it cheap funding and a place to hold deposits [1][2]. Remaining long-term borrowing was about RMB 1.8bn at rates of 1.2–2.34% at Q1 2026 (secondary [7]).
3. **Carbon.** The low-carbon footprint is a potential future advantage. Aluminium smelting has been brought into China's national carbon market, with a start-up phase in 2024–2026 and a deepening phase from 2027 (secondary [14]). The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism also applies from 2026 [47].

On carbon, a dose of scepticism is warranted. The company reported no export revenue in 2024 [2]. Any "green premium" therefore has to come through domestic customers or the carbon market, not through CBAM-exposed export sales. Neither the annual report nor the half-year summary quantifies a price premium for green metal.

### Products and Services

The 2025 annual report lists the main products as [1]:

- **Remelt ingot (重熔用铝锭).** Standard primary aluminium ingot, priced off the Changjiang or SHFE benchmark. The commodity core of the business.
- **Wrought and cast aluminium alloy ingots.** Alloyed metal for extrusion, rolling or casting customers. Alloy capacity is 1.61 Mt [1]. Capacity rose in 2026 after a 50 kt recycled-aluminium line at Yangzonghai was completed [16].
- **Electrical round aluminium rod.** Feedstock for power cable and conductor makers. This links demand to grid investment.
- **Alumina.** Mostly consumed internally; any surplus or traded volume is not separately disclosed.
- **Anode carbon and graphitised cathodes.** Consumables for smelting, largely for internal use. A joint venture (Sotong, 90 kt) adds anode supply [2].
- **Welding materials and smaller products** [1].

The company also states that it is expanding into recycled aluminium, the treatment of electrolytic-aluminium solid waste, and aluminium-ash recycling [1]. These are small next to the core business. Their main value is environmental compliance and the ability to sell a lower-carbon blend.

End markets are described only in general terms. The report links demand to:

- vehicle lightweighting, especially battery parts and wheels for electric vehicles;
- rail transit, aerospace and defence;
- power transmission;
- packaging and consumer electronics;
- energy storage and photovoltaics [1].

No split of sales by end market is given.

### Brand Portfolio

Aluminium is sold to specification, not by brand, and the company has no consumer brands. Its commercial identity rests on three labels:

- **"Green aluminium" (绿色铝).** Used throughout its reporting and backed by product carbon-footprint certification [2].
- **ASI certification.** The company says it is the only Chinese firm holding Aluminium Stewardship Initiative certification across the whole chain from bauxite to processing, under both the performance and chain-of-custody standards [2][7].
- **"National environmentally friendly enterprise" status** [2].

These labels matter mainly to downstream customers who report Scope 3 emissions, such as auto makers, battery makers and packaging firms. Their commercial value is not disclosed.

### Corporate History

**Listing.** Yunnan Aluminium is a long-listed Shenzhen A-share. A broker note reports 22 cash dividends since listing, totalling RMB 6.1bn up to mid-2025 (secondary [14]). The listing year is commonly given as 1998, but the 2024 annual report extract read here does not state it, and it has not been confirmed against a primary document.

**Ownership.** For most of its history the controlling shareholder was Yunnan Metallurgical Group, the provincial metals holding company. In November 2022 the controlling shareholder changed to Chalco [2]. Yunnan Metallurgical kept 13.00%, and both holders now sit under Chinalco Group [1]. The company has therefore moved from being a provincial champion to being one of Chalco's consolidated smelting subsidiaries, while keeping its own Shenzhen listing and minority shareholders. HKSCC Nominees, which holds shares bought through Shenzhen–Hong Kong Stock Connect, held 7.49% at end-2025 and 8.40% at mid-2026 [1][3].

**Financial repair.** Before 2020 the company carried heavy debt. A broker note puts annual finance costs at RMB 0.8–1.0bn before 2020 (secondary [11]). Since then debt has been paid down to the point where net finance cost turned negative in 2025 (secondary [11][14]). The debt-to-asset ratio fell from 23.28% at end-2024 [2] to 19.84% at end-2025 (secondary [7]) and 15.27% at end-September 2026 (secondary [10]).

**Recent capacity and corporate actions:**

- **2024.** Sold a 100 kt electrolytic-capacity quota to Chalco's Qinghai branch for RMB 567.81m, booking a gain of about RMB 166.6m [2]. Capacity quotas are tradable under the national cap (Part 3).
- **June 2025.** A 34,175 t smelting project at Heqing Yixin started up, taking capacity to 3.08 Mt (secondary [15]).
- **October 2025.** Paid RMB 500m in cash for about 16.70% of Yunnan Aluminium Foil, a downstream rolling company in the same industrial park that buys its metal from Yunnan Aluminium (secondary [12]).
- **December 2025.** Bought 28.7425% of its subsidiary Yunlu Yongxin from Yunnan Metallurgical, a related party, for about RMB 1.0bn, funded by redirected IPO proceeds [1].
- **2025 impairment.** Recognised RMB 322.8m of fixed-asset impairment on idle and retired equipment at the parent and four subsidiaries [1].
- **2026.** Completed the 50 kt recycled-aluminium line at Yangzonghai [16]. Added power generation, supply and transmission to the business scope [3].

**Dividend policy.** The payout ratio has risen steadily (Part 4). It was about 12% of profit in 2021–2022 (secondary [13]), 32% in 2024 [2] and about 40% in 2025 and H1 2026 [1][3].

# 2. Company Logistics

## 2.1 Customers and Distribution

Yunnan Aluminium sells primary aluminium and alloy to traders, to downstream fabricators (extruders, rollers, cable makers and wheel casters) and, above all, to its parent Chalco. The 2024 annual report, the latest full report read for this primer, discloses customer concentration but names only the related party [2].

| Customer concentration (2024) | Sales (RMB bn) | % of revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Top five customers combined | 21.28 | 39.07% |
| of which Chalco (related party) | 16.11 | 29.59% |
| Other four customers, each | — | 1.75%–3.35% |
| All other customers | 33.17 | 60.93% |

*Source: [2]. Total 2024 revenue RMB 54.45bn.*

**The Chalco channel.** Almost 30% of revenue goes to Chalco. Chalco is both the controlling shareholder and a large aluminium marketer: its "marketing" segment had revenue of RMB 105.16bn in H1 2026 alone [23]. The likeliest reading is that Chalco's trading arm buys a large share of Yunnan Aluminium's ingot and resells it into the domestic market. This is an inference; the report does not describe the contracts. For the minority shareholder the important question is pricing. The report treats these as related-party transactions priced at market, and it says no more than that [2]. For 2026 the company expects related-party transactions with Chinalco Group and its affiliates of about RMB 51.44bn, excluding tax, covering both sales and purchases. These require shareholder approval, which the related shareholders sit out [1].

**Third-party sales.** The other 70% of revenue goes to a dispersed base. No customer outside Chalco reaches 3.4% of sales [2]. In Yunnan, a growing share of metal is delivered as liquid aluminium or alloy to neighbouring fabricators. Yunnan Aluminium Foil, in which the company bought a 16.7% stake in 2025, sits in the same industrial park as the company's headquarters and buys its main raw material from Yunnan Aluminium (secondary [12]). Electrical rod goes to cable makers, and alloy ingot goes to wheel and die-casting plants. The annual report does not give a channel split between direct sales to fabricators and sales to traders.

**Pricing mechanism.** Sales are priced off public benchmarks: the SHFE contract and Changjiang spot for ingot, plus a processing premium for alloy and rod. Broker notes analyse the company's quarterly results against the Changjiang average price and the national alumina average, and the reported margins move with that spread (secondary [6][8][9]). The company does not disclose whether it hedges. The 2024 report lists aluminium-price volatility as its first risk and does not describe a hedging programme [2]. In practice, therefore, the company's realised price can be treated as the spot benchmark less freight and plus product premia.

**Seasonality.** Sales volume follows production. Production follows power availability, and power is tightest in the dry season, roughly November to May (Part 1). Reported 2025 quarterly revenue was flat across the year: RMB 14.4bn, 14.7bn, 15.0bn and 16.0bn [1]. That was a year without major curtailment. In 2022–23 the seasonality was sharp [32][35].

## 2.2 Suppliers and Procurement

Procurement is more concentrated than sales, and more tied to the parent.

| Supplier concentration (2024) | Purchases (RMB bn) | % of purchases |
|---|---|---|
| Top five suppliers combined | 32.49 | 69.37% |
| of which Chalco (related party) | 23.54 | 50.27% |
| Second-largest supplier (unnamed) | — | 17.75% |
| All other suppliers | — | 30.63% |

*Source: [2].*

**Alumina.** Alumina is the largest input by value. The company refines only about 1.3–1.4 Mt a year in Wenshan [1][4]. Its smelters need roughly 6 Mt, so most alumina is bought in (inference; see Part 1). Chalco, China's largest alumina producer, with 17.35 Mt of metallurgical-grade alumina output in 2025 (secondary [48]), is the obvious and dominant source. Chalco provided half of all purchases by value in 2024 [2]. The 2024 report does not state how much alumina was bought, from whom or at what price [2]. Purchased alumina has to travel to Yunnan by rail and road from refineries in Guangxi, Guizhou and further north, and that freight is a structural cost of being an inland smelter. The size of the cost is not disclosed.

**Electricity.** Electricity is the second-largest input. The unnamed second-largest supplier, at 17.75% of purchases, is probably the provincial grid company or the power-trading counterparty, but the report does not say so [2]. That share is consistent with power costing roughly as much per tonne as alumina (see the illustrative cost table in Part 1), though not all power appears under a single supplier. The company trades in Yunnan's power market on medium-term and annual contracts [2]. A broker models the all-in tariff at about RMB 0.43/kWh including tax (secondary [11]). The company itself does not disclose its tariff.

**Carbon anodes and other inputs.** Anode carbon is largely made in-house at 0.80 Mt a year of capacity, through Yuanxin Carbon and other units, supplemented by the Sotong joint venture (90 kt) [1][2]. The raw materials for anodes, petroleum coke and pitch, are bought in, and the report lists petroleum coke and metallic silicon among its raw-material price risks [2]. Alloying elements such as silicon and magnesium are bought for the alloy lines.

**Bauxite.** The Wenshan refinery runs on local bauxite. Its own mines are supplemented by purchases, and the company does not give a self-sufficiency ratio [2]. The 2025 annual report records that new mining rights at Yangliujing and Dashipen had not been granted [1]. In the near term, then, the company cannot raise its alumina self-supply materially. Its alumina exposure remains a purchase exposure.

**Financial services from the parent.** Procurement of financial services is also concentrated in the group, and the board has set caps on each arrangement [1][2]:

- **Chalco Finance:** a deposit cap of RMB 9bn and a loan cap of RMB 9bn for one year. Separately, the 2024 report cites a RMB 5bn daily deposit cap and an unused RMB 2.2bn credit line with a Chinalco finance entity [2]. The two documents name different entities and caps, and they have not been reconciled here.
- **Chinalco commercial factoring:** a cap of RMB 3bn over three years.
- **Chinalco leasing:** a cap of RMB 0.5bn for 2026–2029.

The board reviews a half-yearly risk assessment of Chalco Finance, with related directors abstaining [3]. The headline cash balance (Part 4) does not show how much of the company's cash sits with group finance entities rather than with commercial banks. That is a disclosure gap worth closing in due diligence.

## 2.3 Geographic Distribution

**Production.** All production is in Yunnan province. The smelters are spread across the province [2]:

- the south-east: Wenshan, Jianshui and Gejiu;
- the north-east: Zhaotong and Fuyuan;
- the centre: Kunming/Yangzonghai and Qujing;
- the north-west: Heqing.

The dispersion spreads exposure across different parts of the provincial grid. It does not remove dry-season risk, because curtailment orders have been province-wide [32][35]. Yunnan was China's fourth-largest aluminium-producing province by 2022. Its output rose 37% that year to 4.2 Mt, as capacity relocated there for hydropower [36]. By end-2025 China Hongqiao had moved 2.18 Mt of capacity into the province [27], and Shenhuo had 0.9 Mt there [29]. Yunnan Aluminium's 3.08 Mt is therefore one of several large claims on the same seasonal power supply (Part 3).

**Sales.** All 2024 revenue, RMB 54.45bn, was domestic. Export revenue was zero, and the report does not split Yunnan from other provinces [2]. A broker note expects the company to absorb more metal locally through downstream projects in the province (secondary [12]). The company points to Yunnan's location as a gateway to South and Southeast Asia, via RCEP and the China–Laos railway, as a competitive strength [2]. Its sales disclosure does not yet show any export business.

**Assets and people.** The company had 10,224 employees at end-2024: 1,240 at the parent and 8,984 in subsidiaries. Of these, 7,959 were production staff [2]. It also funds 1,787 retirees [2]. All fixed assets are in Yunnan. The company has no overseas operations and does not disclose any overseas expansion plans. In this it differs from Chalco, which is building a 1.2 Mt alumina refinery in Guinea [24], and from China Hongqiao and Tianshan, which have Indonesian and Guinean projects [26][31].

# 3. Industry Overview

## 3.1 Industry Overview

**What the industry makes.** The aluminium chain has four steps:

1. **Bauxite** is mined, mostly in Guinea, Australia, China, Brazil and Indonesia.
2. **Alumina** (aluminium oxide) is refined from bauxite, at roughly 2.5–3 tonnes of bauxite per tonne of alumina.
3. **Primary aluminium** is smelted from alumina by electrolysis, at roughly 1.9–2 tonnes of alumina and about 13,000–14,000 kWh of electricity per tonne.
4. The metal is **cast and fabricated** into extrusions, sheet, foil, wire and castings.

Recycled (secondary) aluminium uses about a twentieth of the energy and competes with primary metal in many alloy applications. The ratios above are industry norms. They explain why power and alumina dominate smelter costs, and why where a smelter sits, and what powers it, matters more than almost anything else.

**China's place.** China makes nearly 60% of the world's primary aluminium. Estimates of its exact 2025 output differ by source:

| Estimate of China's 2025 primary aluminium output | Mt | Source and scope |
|---|---|---|
| National Bureau of Statistics-based series | 45.02 | AL Circle [17]; CleanTechnica [46] |
| International Aluminium Institute estimate | 44.2 | CRU, citing IAI [21] |
| China Nonferrous Metals Industry Association (as cited) | 43.70 | Faxian Gongchang report, citing CNIA [22]; unverified |

*The differences reflect data source and scope, for example whether alloy output from captive smelters is included. The same report puts global 2025 primary output at 72.5 Mt (IAI) [22].*

Estimates for H1 2026 also differ: 22.34 Mt, up 2.2% year on year [17][20], against 23.19 Mt, up 3.8% [46]. These are not reconciled here. Either way, national output is running at or slightly above the 45 Mt a year that the capacity cap was meant to allow.

**The 45 Mt cap.** In 2017 the National Development and Reform Commission capped China's electrolytic-aluminium capacity at about 45 Mt a year. The aims were to curb chronic overcapacity, emissions and the power demand of a notoriously power-hungry sector [18][22]. For years the cap did not bind, because operating capacity sat well below it. That has now changed:

- **End-2025:** operating capacity was about 44.2 Mt by CNIA's count (as cited [22]).
- **2026:** SMM puts current operating capacity at about 45.28 Mt [19].
- **August 2026:** a Reuters report cites an estimate that smelters ran at 99.7% of an effective capacity of 45.26 Mt [18].

The government's 2025–2027 action plan for the sector reaffirms the 45 Mt cap. It targets a 30% renewable-energy share in smelting by 2027 and more than 15 Mt a year of recycled aluminium [45]. SMM also reports tighter compliance checks on domestic capacity in 2026 [19].

The cap has three consequences that run through the rest of this primer:

- **Volume growth for Chinese smelters is close to zero.** A producer can grow only by acquiring or relocating existing capacity quotas, by buying out minority interests, or by building abroad. Yunnan Aluminium's 2024 sale of a 100 kt quota to Chalco Qinghai for RMB 567.81m, about RMB 5,700 per tonne of capacity, shows that quotas have a traded value [2].
- **China can no longer act as the world's swing supplier.** When smelters elsewhere shut, Chinese capacity cannot simply restart to fill the gap. A broker note argues this is a structural change, because Chinese utilisation used to sit below 85% (secondary [11]).
- **Margins move to the scarce step.** With smelting capped and alumina capacity still expanding, the margin pool has shifted from refiners to smelters.

**Outside China.** Chinese producers are building smelting capacity abroad, mainly in Indonesia. Reuters counts about 15 Mt of planned Chinese-linked overseas capacity. The consultancy CM Group expects only 9–10 Mt of it to be built, about half in Indonesia [20]. In July 2026 Morgan Stanley forecast that the global market would move from a deficit of 1.1 Mt in 2026 to a surplus of 0.83 Mt in 2027 and 1.8 Mt in 2028 [20]. Against that, 2026 brought supply losses from Middle East smelters affected by regional conflict. China Hongqiao's management expects the effect to persist into H2 2026, and says overseas capacity additions are running slower than planned [26]. Brokers covering Yunnan Aluminium argue that these losses, together with China's 99% utilisation, leave the global market tight into 2027–2028 (secondary [10][11]). These are forecasts by interested parties, and the industry has a long record of surplus forecasts being overtaken by events in both directions.

**Alumina is in surplus.** China's alumina industry is not capped in the same way, and capacity has kept growing. Domestic operating alumina capacity passed 100 Mt a year in 2026 [40]. That is well above what about 45 Mt of smelting needs, roughly 87–90 Mt. China produced 87.6 Mt of alumina in 2025 (as cited [22]). New refineries in Guangxi added further supply in 2026, including a 2.4 Mt a year plant at Fangchenggang, which market participants expect to keep the market in persistent surplus [38]. Prices have fallen as a result. The domestic spot price averaged RMB 2,697/t in H1 2026, down 21.7% year on year [37]. Broker notes put Q3 2026 national averages at about RMB 2,718/t, against a 2024–early-2025 peak of over RMB 3,500/t (secondary [9][13]).

Two policy points limit how far this surplus can be relied on:

- China's dependence on Guinean bauxite is high. Guinea supplied 74.8% of China's bauxite imports in August 2026, and its proposed export-quota system is an upside risk to alumina costs [39].
- A government plan bars new alumina lines that use gibbsite bauxite. Gibbsite is the type found in most of China's domestic deposits [41].

**Demand.** Chinese aluminium demand is spread across construction, transport, power grids, packaging and consumer goods. The mix is shifting away from property and towards electric vehicles, grid build-out, solar frames and energy storage. Yunnan Aluminium's own report makes this point [1], and broker notes say the same (secondary [10]). Neither the company nor the sources consulted gives a reliable end-use split for 2026, so none is shown here. Domestic social inventories of aluminium ingot fell to about 528 kt by end-October 2026, down more than 900 kt since May (secondary [10]).

**Yunnan as a production region.** Yunnan's grid is dominated by hydropower, which supplies roughly 80% of the province's generation [35]. Cheap, low-carbon power drew smelting capacity south-west from coal-powered regions in the late 2010s and early 2020s. Yunnan's output rose 37% in 2022 to 4.2 Mt [36]. China Hongqiao has since relocated 2.18 Mt to the province [27], and Shenhuo runs 0.9 Mt there [29]. The cost of this concentration is seasonal fragility. Smelters use a large share of the province's electricity: about 30% according to 2023 reports [32][35], and about 18% of generation in the winter of 2021–22 according to SMM [49]. In drought years the province has curtailed smelting load in rounds. In 2022–23, operating capacity in Yunnan fell to about 4.04 Mt after the first round and was expected to reach 3.4 Mt after the second [33]. The province has since added wind, solar and thermal generation, and 2025–26 have not seen comparable cuts in the sources consulted. The risk has not gone away; the 2024 annual report still lists it [2].

## 3.2 Industry Competitive Dynamics

**Structure.** Chinese primary aluminium is moderately concentrated. One industry report, citing CNIA, says the top eight producers held 81.4% of electrolytic capacity in 2025 [22]. They are Chalco, China Hongqiao, Yunnan Aluminium, Shenhuo, Tianshan Aluminium, Nanshan Aluminium, East Hope and Xinfa. That figure has not been verified against CNIA itself. **Basis of competition.** In a commodity whose volume is capped, producers compete on cost and on security of inputs, not on price or product. Four things decide where a smelter sits on the cost curve:

- **Power cost and reliability.** Captive coal plants in Xinjiang and Shandong give stable, fairly cheap power with high carbon. Yunnan hydro is cheap and low-carbon, but seasonal and subject to provincial dispatch.
- **Alumina self-sufficiency.** Hongqiao and Chalco are long alumina; Yunnan Aluminium is short. In 2025–26 the short position has been an advantage because alumina prices fell. When alumina is tight it becomes a disadvantage. Chalco's own segment results show the swing: in H1 2026 its primary-aluminium segment profit rose 228% to RMB 26.62bn, while its alumina segment made a loss of RMB 0.82bn [23].
- **Scale and technology.** Unit power consumption and cell technology. Tianshan's 1.4 Mt efficiency upgrade is an example [31].
- **Logistics** to customers, mostly in eastern and southern China.

The cap also redirects competition into a contest for quotas and locations. Producers relocate quotas to cheaper-power regions, as Hongqiao has done from Shandong to Yunnan [27]. They buy minority stakes to raise attributable capacity, as Yunnan Aluminium has [1][6]. They build abroad, as Hongqiao, Tianshan and Chalco are doing in Indonesia and Guinea [20][24][31].

**Where intensity is high and low.** Price competition in ingot is low. Every producer is a price-taker on the SHFE benchmark, and with utilisation near 100% no one is cutting price to fill capacity. Competition for Yunnan's power is the opposite. Yunnan Aluminium, Hongqiao and Shenhuo together have over 6 Mt of smelting in one province whose hydropower is seasonal (from [1][27][29]). In a drought year the provincial government allocates cuts across all of them. Being a provincial incumbent and a Chinalco subsidiary may help in that allocation, but the sources consulted give no evidence of preferential treatment. In 2023, Yunnan Aluminium cut by a similar proportion to its peers [32]. Competition for alumina is low while the surplus lasts.

**Structural trends.** Five trends are shaping the industry:

1. **Decarbonisation.** The 2027 target of a 30% renewable share in smelting [45], the inclusion of aluminium in the national carbon market [14] and the EU's CBAM [47] all favour hydro-based producers, at least on paper. How much of this turns into a cash premium for Yunnan Aluminium is unproven. It sells only in China [2], and China's carbon price has historically been low.
2. **Relocation and overseas growth.** Capacity is moving to cheaper-power regions at home and to Indonesia abroad [20]. This will add supply outside China and erode China's export dominance in semi-finished products over time.
3. **Recycling.** The target of more than 15 Mt a year of recycled aluminium by 2027 [45] slows the growth of demand for primary metal. It also gives producers like Yunnan Aluminium, with its 50 kt line [16], a way to grow output without new smelting quota.
4. **Alumina oversupply and bauxite risk.** Domestic alumina capacity far exceeds smelting needs, which favours smelters while it lasts. Guinea's export policy is the main risk to that balance [38][39][41].
5. **Shareholder returns.** Listed aluminium producers are raising payouts. Tianshan has committed to at least 50% for 2026 [31], Chalco paid about 40% at the interim stage [23], and Yunnan Aluminium also pays about 40% [3]. The industry's cash generation at current spreads is far above its capped capital needs.

**How management frames competition, and the caveat.** Yunnan Aluminium presents its competitiveness as green power, a fully integrated chain, ASI certification, its location near Southeast Asia and the backing of Chinalco [2]. The integration claim deserves care. The company refines less than a quarter of the alumina it uses and does not disclose bauxite self-sufficiency [1][2][4]. Its integration is mostly downstream, into alloy and rod, not upstream. "Green" is a real cost and carbon advantage in wet years and a curtailment risk in dry ones. The Chinalco link brings cheap finance and an assured alumina supply, but it also routes about 30% of sales and 50% of purchases through related-party contracts whose pricing is not set out in detail [2].

## 4.4 Long-Term Guidance and Outlook

**What management guides.** The company gives operating targets, not financial guidance. For 2026 the targets are alumina output of about 1.30 Mt, aluminium products of 3.19 Mt and carbon products of 0.75 Mt (secondary [4][7]). It publishes no revenue, profit, margin or capex guidance and no medium-term financial targets in the documents read. Its strategic aims, as stated, are four [1][2]:

- keep its "green aluminium" integrated model and raise the clean-power share;
- secure more bauxite through exploration and the conversion of exploration rights;
- extend into alloy, recycled aluminium and solid-waste recovery;
- benefit from its location for trade with South and Southeast Asia.

4. **Alumina supply.** New Guangxi refineries on one side; Guinea's bauxite export policy on the other.
5. **The payout ratio.** Whether the board lifts it above 40% as cash accumulates, and how much cash is kept with Chinalco Finance.
6. **Related-party pricing.** The terms of sales to and purchases from Chalco, and any further minority purchases from group entities.
7. **Upkeep.** Whether capex rises towards depreciation as potlines age.
