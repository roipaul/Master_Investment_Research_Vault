# 1. Company Overview

## 1.1 Tencent Holdings (0700.HK) — Company Primer

> **Source limitations.** Tencent's investor-relations site serves a bot-check page to automated readers, so the results presentations and the Q3 2026 HKEX announcement could not be read directly. This primer is built from the 2025 Annual Report [40] (supplied from Paul's files), the HKEX results announcements for FY2024, FY2025 and H1 2026 [1][4][9], the quarterly earnings releases [2][5][7][10] and call transcripts [3][6][8]. Corporate history before 2018 rests on secondary sources [13][14][15], and some Q3 2026 cost detail comes from the call [3] and from broker results notes [41][42], labelled where used.

### Company Overview

Tencent is the company behind Weixin (called WeChat outside mainland China), the messaging and services app that 1.45 billion people use each month, and it makes money from that audience in four ways: selling items inside video games, selling advertising, taking small fees on payments and financial products, and renting computing power and software to businesses [2]. It is incorporated in the Cayman Islands, listed in Hong Kong since June 2004 (stock code 700 in Hong Kong dollars and 80700 in renminbi) and headquartered in Shenzhen; it mainly operates in mainland China [1][13][40]. It had 9,120,235,999 shares in issue at the end of 2025 [40]. Ma Huateng (Pony Ma), one of the five founders, is Chairman and CEO; Martin Lau is President, James Mitchell Chief Strategy Officer and John Lo Chief Financial Officer. No succession has been announced. The same four executives have presented results since at least 2018 [3][6][8].

The scale: revenue of RMB 751.8bn in 2025, up 14%, and non-IFRS operating profit of RMB 280.7bn, a 37% margin [1]. The company had 115,849 employees at the end of 2025 [1]. Weixin and WeChat had 1,449m combined monthly active users (MAU) and QQ, the older desktop-era messenger, had 533m mobile MAU at 30 September 2026; paid subscriptions to video, music and other content averaged 253m a day in Q3 2026 [2]. Besides its operating businesses Tencent holds minority stakes in other companies: listed stakes worth RMB 505.4bn at fair value and unlisted stakes carried at RMB 397.2bn at 30 September 2026 [2][3].

The model in one paragraph: Tencent owns China's main communication network and uses it as a distribution layer that it monetises several times over. Users pay directly for games and subscriptions; merchants pay for attention (advertising) and for transactions (payments and e-commerce service fees); fund houses and banks pay for distribution of wealth and loan products; and enterprises pay for cloud infrastructure and, increasingly, AI services. Most costs are fixed (staff, servers, content) or a pass-through share of revenue (app-store fees, payment-bank fees, revenue shares with game developers and creators), so revenue growth in the higher-margin lines lifts margins.

Management's current framing, set out on the Q2 2026 call, is that Tencent should be read as two businesses: "existing core franchises" that grow "with quite a bit of operating leverage", and a "new AI native business" (its Hunyuan foundation models, the Yuanbao chatbot, the WorkBuddy and CodeBuddy productivity agents, and the Xiaowei agent inside Weixin) whose operating losses are disclosed separately [6]. On the Q4 2025 call Martin Lau also set out six "moats" he believes protect the core: network effects, integration with physical supply chains, licensing requirements, scarce intellectual property, low take rates relative to value, and closed private data [8].

### Segments

Tencent reports four segments. Value-Added Services (VAS) is further split into domestic games, international games and social networks. There has been no change to segment definitions in the last two years. Two disclosure changes matter for comparisons: from Q1 2026 Tencent reports non-IFRS operating profit "excluding new AI products", and in the Q3 2026 release it defined those products as Hy (Hunyuan), Yuanbao, CodeBuddy, WorkBuddy and Xiaowei [2][7]. Games that earn money from in-app advertising (for example Miniclip's *Arrows – Puzzle Escape*) are booked in marketing services, not international games, which flattered advertising growth by about 2 percentage points in Q2 2026 [6].

**Value-Added Services** sells digital goods directly to consumers. *Domestic games* is revenue from games played in mainland China, whether developed by Tencent (Honour of Kings, Peacekeeper Elite, Delta Force) or licensed from others and published by Tencent (Dungeon & Fighter from Nexon, VALORANT from its own subsidiary Riot). *International games* is revenue from games outside China, mostly from studios Tencent owns or controls (Riot Games, Supercell, Miniclip, Funcom and others); it passed US$10bn in 2025 [1]. *Social networks* is subscriptions to Tencent Video, Tencent Music (a consolidated, separately listed subsidiary), and paid features and virtual items inside QQ and Weixin, including items sold in Weixin mini-games [1][6]. VAS has the highest margin in the group, and the margin has risen as internally developed games have grown faster than licensed ones: 54% in 2023, 57% in 2024, 60% in 2025 and 64% in Q2 and Q3 2026 [1][3][4][9].

**Marketing Services** is advertising sold across Weixin (Moments, Video Accounts, Official Accounts, Mini Programs, Search), QQ, Tencent Video, Tencent News and third-party apps through Tencent's ad network. It was 19% of revenue in 2025, growing faster than the group, and its gross margin rose from 51% in 2023 to 58% in 2025 [1][9]. In Q3 2026 its margin fell to 52% as Tencent began paying for mini-drama content and AI infrastructure costs [3].

**FinTech and Business Services (FBS)** combines two different businesses. *FinTech services* is WeChat Pay commercial payment fees, distribution fees on wealth-management products sold through Licaitong, and fees from consumer loans made by WeBank (an associate) through Weilidai. *Business services* is Tencent Cloud (infrastructure, platform and software, now including GPU rental, Model-as-a-Service and WorkBuddy subscriptions), e-commerce technology service fees charged to Mini Shops merchants, and other enterprise software such as WeCom and Tencent Meeting [1][7]. FBS is the largest segment by revenue but the lowest-margin of the three main ones; its gross margin rose from 40% in 2023 to 51% in 2025 as low-margin cloud projects were exited and wealth management grew [1][9].

**Others** is mainly investment in, production and distribution of films and television programmes for third parties, and copyright licensing; it is small and loss-making at the gross level in 2026 [1][4].

### How the Business Model Works

#### Where revenue comes from, mechanically

*Games.* Almost all of Tencent's games are free to play. Players buy virtual items (character skins, weapons, battle passes, draws for random items) with real money. Tencent records the cash received as *gross receipts* and recognises revenue over the estimated life of the item or of the paying player, so reported revenue lags cash by one or two quarters; the unrecognised balance sits in deferred revenue, which was RMB 129.4bn (current portion) at 30 September 2026 against RMB 110.3bn at the end of 2025 [1][2]. This is why Q1 2026 domestic games revenue grew only 6% while gross receipts grew at a "teens" rate: the Spring Festival fell later in 2026 and its spending was recognised in later quarters [7]. For games Tencent publishes but did not develop, revenue is recorded gross and the developer's share is a cost; this is why the mix of in-house versus licensed games moves VAS gross margin [1][3].

*Social networks.* Monthly or annual subscriptions (Tencent Video VIP, QQ Music, Super QQ), recognised over the subscription period, plus sales of virtual items in social apps and mini-games. Long-form video subscription revenue fell 6% in Q2 2026 even though Tencent had the top drama series of the quarter [6].

*Advertising.* Ads are sold mainly by auction on a cost-per-click or cost-per-thousand-impressions basis. Revenue is the product of three things: the number of ad impressions (user time spent multiplied by *ad load*, the share of feed slots that carry ads), the price per thousand impressions (eCPM), and the mix of placements [2][6]. Tencent's ad load on Video Accounts has been deliberately kept below peers'; the Q4 2025 presentation said that better AI ad targeting gives "better user experience at equivalent ad load (enable us to increase Video Accounts ad load at measured pace)" [11]. In Q3 2026 Video Accounts ad impressions grew more than 50% while time spent grew more than 20%, so load rose [3].

*Payments.* WeChat Pay charges merchants a percentage of transaction value. Consumer-to-consumer transfers (red packets, splitting a bill) are free; withdrawals to bank cards above a threshold carry a small fee. Martin Lau has described mobile payment take rates in China as "already among the lowest in the world" [8]. Revenue is booked gross; fees paid to banks and clearing networks are in cost of revenue (part of the RMB 136.4bn of "transaction costs" in 2025, which also include app-store and distribution channel fees) [1].

*Wealth management and loans.* Tencent does not take balance-sheet risk on most of these products. Licaitong earns a distribution fee on assets held in money-market, bond and index funds managed by third-party fund houses. Weilidai loans are made by WeBank, in which Tencent holds a minority stake, and Tencent earns a service fee [1][6].

*Cloud and business services.* Infrastructure is billed on usage (compute hours, storage, bandwidth) or by contract; software (WeCom, Tencent Meeting, Docs) on subscription; GPU rental by the hour; Model-as-a-Service by tokens consumed; and WorkBuddy mostly on subscription, so cash leads revenue [3]. E-commerce technology service fees are a percentage of Mini Shops gross merchandise value (GMV) [7].

#### Cost structure and unit economics

Tencent's 2025 costs by nature, as disclosed in the annual results, show what the margins are made of [1]:

| Cost line, FY2025 (RMB bn) | Amount | % of revenue | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|

| Employee benefits (incl. share-based compensation of 31.9) | 130.7 | 17.4% | Mostly fixed |
| Content costs (excl. amortisation) | 69.6 | 9.3% | Semi-fixed (video, music, live-streaming, mini-drama) |
| Bandwidth and server custody | 32.0 | 4.3% | Semi-variable with usage |
| Depreciation and amortisation (EBITDA basis) | 66.0 | 8.8% | Fixed once invested |
| Selling and marketing (income statement) | 41.7 | 5.6% | Discretionary |

*Source: [1]. Lines overlap the income-statement presentation and do not sum to total costs.*

Three features follow. First, the largest single cost is variable and roughly proportional to revenue in games and payments, so it does not dilute margin as those lines grow. Second, staff and depreciation are largely fixed: revenue grew 23% from 2023 to 2025 while headcount grew 10% (105,417 to 115,849) [1][9]. Third, mix drives margin. In-house games pay no developer share, so they earn more than licensed games; advertising sold on Tencent's own traffic has no traffic-acquisition cost beyond revenue shares with creators and Official Account owners; wealth management has almost no marginal cost; payments carry bank and network fees; long-form video carries content amortisation. The rise in group gross margin from 48% (2023) to 56% (2025) came largely from this mix shift and from exiting low-margin cloud contracts [1][9].

The cost base changed in 2026. R&D rose 42% to RMB 32.4bn in Q3 2026 to fund Hunyuan models and AI capabilities [3]. Depreciation of property, plant and equipment, investment properties and right-of-use assets was RMB 11.2bn in Q2 2026 alone, against RMB 32.8bn for all of 2025 [1][4]. Management said memory chip (DRAM) prices rose about 400% year on year in early 2026 and weighed on the cost of running existing products [3].

#### The demand engine

Demand rests on Weixin's reach and time spent, which is close to saturated in China (1.45bn MAU, +2% a year) [2]. Growth therefore comes from more time and more monetisation per user rather than more users:

- **Games** depend on a handful of long-running "evergreen" titles. Honour of Kings launched in 2015 and still set lifetime-high quarterly gross receipts in Q1 2026, alongside Peacekeeper Elite and Delta Force [7]. Delta Force passed 50m peak daily active users (DAU) in February 2026 [8]. Game spending has historically been less sensitive to the economy than to content cadence and regulation (Part 3).
- **Advertising** depends on Video Accounts time spent (+20% a year through 2025 and 2026), ad load, and advertiser demand, which tracks China consumption. Management described the ad market as weak "for several months" in 2026 [3].
- **Payments** follow consumer spending; in 2026 the number of transactions grew while the value per transaction fell [6].
- **Wealth management** grows with household savings shifting from deposits into funds [6].
- **Cloud** depends on enterprise IT budgets and, since 2025, on AI compute demand; it has been capacity-constrained in 2026 [6].

Recurring versus discretionary: subscriptions, cloud contracts and payment volume are recurring; in-game spending is discretionary per transaction but behaves as recurring in aggregate for evergreen titles; advertising is the most cyclical line. Tencent does not quantify the split.

#### The reinforcing structure

The mechanism, without the marketing, is that one daily habit — messaging in Weixin — lowers the cost of acquiring customers for every other product to near zero, and each product adds data or transactions that make the others work better:

1. **Social graph → games.** Games such as Honour of Kings are played with friends from Weixin and QQ, and new games are distributed through Weixin's game centre and mini-games, so Tencent's user acquisition cost for games is lower than a standalone publisher's.
2. **Time spent → advertising inventory.** Video Accounts, Moments and Search sit inside an app opened many times a day. Video Accounts became the second-largest short-video service in China by DAU in 2025 [8].
3. **Payments → closed-loop advertising.** Because WeChat Pay is inside Weixin, an ad on Video Accounts can lead to a Mini Shop or mini-program purchase without leaving the app. Advertisers can see the sale, so they bid more. AIM+, Tencent's automated campaign tool, powered about 30% of ad spending by Q1 2026 and can now pick products, create ads and set bids for Mini Shop merchants [6][7].
4. **Mini programs → merchants → fintech.** Mini programs run third-party services (food ordering, ride-hailing, utilities) inside Weixin; each generates payment volume and, increasingly, ad demand from mini-game and mini-drama studios [6].
5. **AI as an amplifier.** Management's claim is that AI improves each step (better ad targeting, game production, search) and that Xiaowei, an agent inside Weixin, could execute tasks across mini programs and merchants [6]. This is a stated intention; Xiaowei had 30m DAU in Q3 2026 and no disclosed revenue [3].

The weakness of the structure is that it is domestic: the reinforcement works only where Weixin is the default communication tool, which is mainland China.

#### Go-to-market

Consumers come through Weixin and QQ organically; Tencent's selling and marketing expense was only 5.6% of revenue in 2025, mostly game launch marketing and, in 2026, promotion of AI products [1][3]. Advertising is sold through an in-house sales force for large brands and through agencies and self-serve tools for smaller advertisers. Games are distributed through Tencent's own channels in China and, on Android, through phone makers' app stores, which take a share of receipts; on iOS through Apple [8]. Cloud is sold by a direct enterprise sales force and partners.

#### Capital model

Two features are unusual. First, Tencent is both an operator and a large investor: its minority stakes (listed RMB 505.4bn, unlisted RMB 397.2bn at September 2026) are worth more than three years of its 2025 non-IFRS net profit, and it has at times returned stakes to shareholders as dividends in kind (JD.com in 2021, Meituan in 2022) [2][19][20]. Second, in 2026 it began paying suppliers in advance for computing capacity ("compute prepayments"), which made free cash flow negative and moved the balance sheet from net cash of RMB 146.9bn at March 2026 to net debt of RMB 13.7bn at September 2026 [2][7]. Its debt is a mix of bank borrowings (RMB 298.9bn at June 2026) and US-dollar notes (RMB 154.0bn) [4].

New products and traction, dated:

- **Hunyuan models.** Hy3 preview launched April 2026 and ranked first on OpenRouter by token usage from 28 April; the production version in July delivered about six times the preview's daily token usage and ranked in the global top 3 on OpenRouter from 7 July [5][6][7]. ### Corporate History

The history is thinly covered in the primary documents read; the founding facts come from secondary sources [13].

- **1998–2004: founding and listing.** Tencent was founded in Shenzhen on 7 November 1998 by Ma Huateng, Zhang Zhidong, Xu Chenye, Chen Yidan and Zeng Liqing; QQ (first called OICQ) launched in February 1999 [13]. The South African media group Naspers invested in 2001; its holding, now held through Prosus, has been the largest single stake ever since [13]. Tencent listed in Hong Kong in June 2004 [13].
- **2003–2016: games become the profit engine.** Tencent began licensing games around 2004 and developing its own from 2007 [13]. It took control of Riot Games (later wholly owned) and in June 2016 bought 84% of Supercell from SoftBank for about US$8.6bn, valuing Supercell at US$10.2bn [14]. These deals created the international games sub-segment.
- **2011–2013: Weixin and WeChat Pay.** Weixin launched in 2011 and WeChat Pay in 2013 [13]. Weixin replaced QQ as the core platform and created the payments and advertising businesses.
- **2018: reorganisation toward enterprise.** On 30 September 2018 Tencent reorganised into business groups, creating the Cloud and Smart Industries Group and the Platform and Content Group, its first major restructuring in six years [15].
- **2021–2023: regulation and the return of capital.** Rules limiting minors to three hours of online games a week took effect in August 2021 [17]. Tencent distributed most of its JD.com stake (about US$16.4bn) to shareholders in December 2021 and most of its Meituan stake (about US$20bn) in November 2022 [19][20]. The People's Bank of China fined Tencent and Tenpay about RMB 2.99bn in July 2023 for past payment-business breaches, closing the fintech rectification [16]. Draft rules in December 2023 proposed limits on in-game spending incentives; Tencent and NetEase shares fell sharply on the day [18]. (The rules were not enacted in that form; approvals have since run at multi-year highs — Part 3.)
- **2024–2026: buybacks, the US list and AI.** Tencent repurchased HK$112.0bn of shares in 2024 and HK$80.0bn in 2025 [1][9]. The US Department of Defense added Tencent to its Section 1260H list of "Chinese military companies" on 6 January 2025; Tencent called this a mistake and said its business would be unaffected [21]. From 2026 Tencent separated its AI-native businesses for disclosure, raised capital spending sharply, and cut the pace of buybacks [2][6][8].

**Ownership and pay (31 December 2025).** Prosus (through MIH Internet Holdings B.V., controlled by Naspers) held 2,079,512,000 shares, 22.80%; it has been selling steadily since 2022 to fund its own buybacks [22][40]. Ma Huateng held 804,859,700 shares, 8.82%, through Advance Data Services, including 95,000,000 held through the Ma Huateng Global Foundation [40]. No other holder was above the disclosure threshold. Ma's 2025 pay was RMB 48.5m (salary RMB 7.6m, bonus RMB 39.4m, no share-based pay), up from RMB 45.1m [40]; his stake is worth well over a thousand times his annual pay. The executives below board level are paid mostly in shares: the twelve senior managers other than the CEO received RMB 4.46bn in 2025, of which RMB 3.92bn share-based, and seven of them were above HK$300m each [40]. The five highest-paid individuals (none of them directors) received RMB 7.59bn, including a one-off RMB 4bn share-based charge from restructuring a commercial arrangement at an overseas subsidiary; the top individual was in the HK$3.34bn band [40]. Martin Lau, President, is not a director and his pay is not separately disclosed.

# 2. Company Logistics

## 2.1 Customers and Distribution

### Who Are the Customers

Tencent has four kinds of paying customer, and they map onto its segments: consumers who buy games and subscriptions; advertisers; merchants and financial institutions that pay for payment processing and product distribution; and enterprises that buy cloud and software. No single customer accounts for 10% of revenue; the documents read name no customer concentration [1].

| Customer group | What they buy | Segment | FY2025 revenue (RMB bn) | % of total | FY2025 growth |
|---------|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| Consumers (players) | Virtual items in domestic and international games | VAS — games | 241.6 | 32% | +22% |
| Consumers (subscribers, social users) | Video, music, literature subscriptions; virtual items in social apps and mini-games | VAS — social networks | 127.7 | 17% | +5% |
| Advertisers | Ads in Weixin, QQ, Tencent Video, news and the ad network | Marketing Services | 145.0 | 19% | +19% |
| Merchants, fund houses, lenders, enterprises | Payment processing, wealth and loan distribution, cloud, enterprise software, e-commerce service fees | FinTech and Business Services | 229.4 | 31% | +8% |
| Others | Film and TV production, copyright | Others | 8.1 | 1% | +4% |

*Sources: [1]. Games total is domestic RMB 164.2bn plus international RMB 77.4bn.*

**Consumers.** The base is Weixin's 1,449m MAU and QQ's 533m mobile MAU at September 2026 [2]. Paid content subscriptions averaged 253m a day in Q3 2026, down 5% year on year, as Tencent Video subscriptions declined [2][6]; in Q3 2025 Tencent Video had 114m subscriptions and Tencent Music 126m [10]. Tencent does not disclose the number of paying game players or average revenue per paying user. Game spending is concentrated: industry practice and management commentary point to a small share of players producing most gross receipts, but Tencent does not quantify it. Minors' play is restricted by regulation to three hours a week (Part 3) [17].

**Advertisers.** On the Q2 2026 call management said most major categories increased spending, including e-commerce, internet services and local services, and that mini-game and mini-drama studios were a growing source of demand [6]. In 2025, e-commerce ad growth slowed as platforms moved budgets into consumer subsidies, and financial-services ads were hit by changes to online lending rules [8]. Smaller advertisers are increasingly served by AIM+, which handled about 30% of ad spending by Q1 2026 [7]; about 80% of mini-drama publishers' monetisation ran through Tencent's AI tools in Q3 2026 [3]. Tencent does not disclose advertiser counts or the share from its top advertisers.

**Merchants and financial institutions.** WeChat Pay's paying customers are merchants; the documents read give no merchant count. Commercial payment volume growth accelerated in Q1 2026, transaction counts grew in Q2 2026 while value per transaction fell less than before [6][7]. Wealth management customers are the fund houses whose products are sold on Licaitong; aggregated customer assets increased year on year in Q2 2026, helped by automated investment strategies and thematic index funds [6].

**Enterprises.** Tencent Cloud serves internet companies (including games and video businesses), financial institutions, government and, increasingly, AI users. Management named an Indonesian telecom operator as an example of an international migration customer [6]. Tencent Cloud reached operating break-even in 2024 and RMB 5bn of adjusted operating profit in 2025 [8].

## 2.2 Suppliers and Procurement

Tencent does not name suppliers or disclose supplier concentration. The cost lines in Part 1 identify where money goes [1]:

- **App stores and payment channels.** "Transaction costs" of RMB 136.4bn in 2025 (18% of revenue) are the largest single cost and include app-store commissions, phone-maker revenue shares, and bank and clearing fees for WeChat Pay [1]. These are percentage-of-revenue costs set by Apple, Android distributors and the payment-clearing system; Tencent negotiates them but does not control them.
- **Game developers.** For licensed games (Dungeon & Fighter from Nexon is the largest named; the PC publishing deal was extended in 2026), Tencent pays the developer a share of revenue, recorded in cost of revenue. In-house development costs sit in staff costs and R&D.
- **Content.** Content costs (excluding amortisation) were RMB 69.6bn in 2025 and RMB 36.1bn in H1 2026: video licences and productions, music rights, live-streaming revenue shares, and since 2026 mini-drama content [1][3][4]. Management said mini-drama content costs were front-loaded and lowered the advertising gross margin in Q3 2026 [3].
- **Computing infrastructure.** Bandwidth and server custody cost RMB 32.0bn in 2025; in 2026 Tencent reports "technical infrastructure operations costs" (RMB 25.9bn in H1 2026) [1][4]. Capital spending on servers, GPUs and data centres was RMB 79.2bn in 2025 and RMB 166.1bn (accrual basis) in the first nine months of 2026 [1][2][4][7].

**Procurement model for AI compute.** In 2026 Tencent moved to long-term contracts with prepayments for computing capacity. Management said Q2 and Q3 prepayments were "abnormally high", that long-term contracts are now in place, and that prepayment outflows should fall [3]. Cash capital spending of about RMB 65bn a quarter is described as "at peak levels" and expected to fluctuate around that level through 2027 before declining [3]. Management said in August 2026 that some compute ordered months earlier could be resold at more than 30% above the price paid [6], and in November that compute pricing would stay "elevated" in 2026–27 and fall "quite significantly" by 2028 [3].

**Input-cost exposure.** The disclosed sensitivities are qualitative:
- *Memory chips.* DRAM prices rose about 400% year on year in early 2026; management said core products absorbed part of the increase and that memory prices had since flattened [3].
- *GPUs and export controls.* Tencent has said US restrictions limit access to advanced GPUs. In November 2026 it said new US restrictions on remote access to advanced chips would slow model training in the short run but not substantially hurt cloud revenue, because inference can run on efficient models and domestic chips; high-end GPUs "cannot actually" be offered to cloud customers anymore [3].
- *Pass-through.* Tencent raised Tencent Cloud prices across the board in May 2026 and cut discounts, citing memory costs [6]. In games and advertising there is no direct pass-through mechanism.

**Trade exposure.** Tencent sells digital services, so tariffs on goods do not apply directly. Its exposure to US–China policy runs through chip export controls (above) and the Section 1260H designation of January 2025, which does not impose sanctions but can affect US counterparties' willingness to deal with it [21].

No commodity-level procurement detail, named supplier shares or contract expiry dates are disclosed in the documents read.

# 3. Industry Overview

## 3.1 Industry Overview

### Introduction

Tencent does not sit in one industry. It competes in at least five Chinese markets — online games, internet advertising, short video and social media (where advertising inventory is created), digital payments and wealth distribution, and cloud computing — plus the global games market and, since 2025, a sixth market for AI models and applications. What ties them together in China is that the country's consumer internet is organised around a few "super-apps" owned by a few groups (Tencent, ByteDance, Alibaba, and to a lesser extent Meituan, PDD, Kuaishou, Baidu and JD.com). Each group owns traffic and tries to monetise it in as many ways as possible, so the same companies meet as competitors in several markets at once.

The economic reason the structure exists is that consumer internet services have high fixed costs (engineering, content, servers) and near-zero marginal costs, and most of them get more useful as more people use them. That favours a few large platforms. In China the effect is stronger than in the West because mobile came before the PC for most users, so one app (Weixin) took over messaging, payments and services at the same time, and because the government's rules on licensing (games, payments, content) raise the cost of entry.

This section describes each market on its own terms and then the competition between the groups. Tencent is used as the example where helpful.

### How the Industry Works

**Games.** Developers make games; publishers operate them, run the servers, sell virtual items and market them; distributors (app stores, phone makers, PC clients, Weixin) take a share of receipts. In China every game that charges money needs a licence from the National Press and Publication Administration (NPPA), and foreign games need a Chinese publisher, which is why Tencent and NetEase act as publishers for Riot, Nexon, Blizzard and others. Nearly all revenue is free-to-play: a large majority of players pay nothing and a small minority pays most of the money. The best games become "evergreens" that run for a decade with regular updates; most new games fail. Margins for the publisher depend on whether it owns the game (no developer share) and the distribution channel (Android phone-maker stores in China have historically taken a larger cut than Apple).

**Advertising.** Platforms with user time sell ads by auction. The industry has shifted from brand display ads to performance ads that are paid for by clicks or conversions, and in China it has shifted further to "closed-loop" ads, where the platform also hosts the shop and the payment (Douyin's live-commerce, Alibaba's Taobao, Tencent's Mini Shops). Closed-loop ads command higher prices because the advertiser can measure the sale. Platforms differ in how much ad load users will tolerate: short-video feeds carry more ads than messaging apps.

**Short video and content.** User time has moved to short video: short-form accounted for 79.1% of mobile video time in Q1 2026, and Douyin alone for 44.3% of mobile video app time [30]. Micro-dramas (minute-long serial episodes) reached 626m users by June 2025 [31] and are a new source of both content spending and advertising demand.

**Payments and wealth.** Third-party payment companies (Alipay, Tenpay/WeChat Pay and smaller players) process payments between consumers and merchants through bank accounts, cleared via NetsUnion. Take rates are low and regulated; profit comes less from payments themselves than from what payments enable — distributing money-market and index funds, consumer loans made by partner banks, and data that improves advertising. Since 2021 regulators have required fintech platforms to hold licences, separate credit data and hold capital, and in 2023 fined Ant Group RMB 7.1bn and Tencent/Tenpay RMB 2.99bn, closing the rectification [16].

**Cloud.** Providers buy servers, chips and data-centre capacity and sell computing, storage and software by usage or contract. It is capital-intensive and price-competitive; profit depends on scale, utilisation and the mix of higher-value software. Since 2025 demand has been driven by AI training and inference, and by GPU rental.

**AI models and applications.** Labs train foundation models (very high fixed compute cost) and sell access by tokens through APIs, or through their own apps. In China, token prices are low; Tencent's view is that "domestic token manufacturing costs are also extremely low" [6]. The leading consumer chatbot is ByteDance's Doubao [29].

### Key Industry Trends and Developments

**1. An AI capital-spending race.** Chinese platforms are spending heavily on AI compute. Alibaba's capital spending was RMB 50.5bn in the September 2026 quarter, up 60%, with free cash flow of −RMB 34.5bn [38]; Tencent's cash capital spending was RMB 65.1bn in the same quarter, up 225% [2]; ByteDance was reported to be on track for about US$50bn of profit in 2025 while shifting spending to AI [39]. Cloud revenue is accelerating with it: Alibaba's cloud and compute services +53% and Tencent Cloud +35% in that quarter [3][38]. Memory chip prices rose about 400% in early 2026 [3].

**2. Time shifting to short video and micro-dramas.** Douyin holds 44.3% of mobile video app time [30]; Tencent's Video Accounts is second by DAU but, according to Tencent, its time per DAU is "less than half" that of the leaders [3][8]. Micro-dramas are a fast-growing format that brings both content costs and new advertisers (mini-drama studios) [3][31].

**3. Closed-loop commerce and AI ad tools.** Platforms are pushing advertisers into in-app shops and automated buying tools. Tencent's AIM+ and Mini Shops, Douyin's live commerce and Alibaba's tools all aim to measure the sale and raise ad prices. Alibaba's customer management revenue (its main ad and commission line) fell 5% in the September 2026 quarter on an accounting change for merchant subsidies, or rose 1% without it [38], showing how e-commerce subsidies and advertising compete for the same merchant budgets.

**4. Games: approvals high, mobile mature, PC growing.** China's licence approvals in 2025 were the most since 2018 [26]. Globally, PC grew 12% in 2025, the fastest rate Newzoo has recorded for PC, while console grew 2.8% [25]. In China, PC and cross-platform titles (Delta Force, VALORANT, NetEase's Where Winds Meet) are the newest large hits. NetEase's games revenue fell 1.7% in Q3 2026 [36].

**5. A weak consumer.** Third-party payment volume grew only 2.8% in 2025 [33]. Tencent reports falling value per payment transaction and a weak ad market in 2026 [3][6]. Wealth-management platforms, by contrast, are growing as households move savings from deposits into funds [6].

**6. Consumer AI apps as a new front.** Standalone AI apps had 499m MAU by mid-2026, up 85% [28]. ByteDance's Doubao leads with 382m MAU, followed by Alibaba's Qwen (167m) and DeepSeek (130m) [29]. Tencent's Yuanbao and Alibaba's Qwen together bought more than 68% of AI-app advertising placements in H1 2026, a sign that user acquisition in this market is being paid for [28].

**7. Chips and geopolitics.** US export controls limit Chinese companies' access to the most advanced GPUs and, from 2026, to remote access to them. Tencent says inference can run on domestic chips but training will be slower [3].

### Level of Competitive Intensity

**Messaging and social: low.** There is no domestic challenger to Weixin's communication graph. Competition is for time inside and outside it (short video, AI chatbots), not for the network itself.

**Games: moderate, and rising at the top.** Tencent and NetEase together hold roughly two-thirds of Chinese game spending (estimate above). Barriers to scale are high — the cost of building and running a large multiplayer game, licences, and distribution — but barriers to entry for small games are falling: management said AI tools produced copycat hyper-casual games in 2026, which model providers then curtailed [3]. MiHoYo (Genshin Impact) and Kuro (Wuthering Waves, part-owned by Tencent) show that hits can come from outside the duopoly. NetEase's games revenue fell in Q3 2026 while Tencent's domestic games grew 11%, a reminder that share moves with individual titles [2][36].

**Advertising: high.** ByteDance, Alibaba, PDD, Kuaishou, Baidu and Tencent all sell to the same merchants, and e-commerce platforms can redirect merchant budgets into subsidies [8][38]. Tencent's advantage is that its inventory is under-monetised (low load), so it can grow by adding supply rather than by cutting price; its disadvantage is that most closed-loop commerce still happens on Douyin and Taobao.

**Payments: low on share, high on price.** WeChat Pay and Alipay form a duopoly that regulators will not let either side end, and take rates are among the lowest in the world [8]. UnionPay's Quick Pass grew MAU nearly 80% in 2025 [31]. Competition shows up in incentives rather than share shifts.

**Cloud: high on price, easing.** China's cloud market has been a price war for years. In 2026 rising memory costs and AI demand allowed price increases: Tencent raised prices across the board in May 2026 and cut discounts, and said the pricing environment "is not as difficult as it has been in the past" [6]. Alibaba's 37% share and its own chips (T-Head) give it scale advantages [32][38].

**AI models and apps: very high.** Several well-funded labs (ByteDance, Alibaba, DeepSeek, Moonshot, MiniMax, Zhipu, Baidu, Tencent) compete on capability and price; API prices are low, and consumer apps are buying users with advertising [6][28]. Tencent's model ranked first among Chinese models on Agent Arena at Hy4's launch, but its consumer chatbot trails [2][29].

| Dimension | What it means here | Who it favours |
|---|---|---|
| Network and daily habit | Being the app opened by default for messaging and payments | Tencent (Weixin); Alibaba in commerce payments |
| Time spent and feed algorithms | Recommendation quality that keeps users watching | ByteDance (Douyin), then Kuaishou and Tencent |
| Content and IP | Owning games, characters, dramas and music users want | Tencent and NetEase in games; ByteDance and Tencent in micro-drama and video |
| Closed-loop commerce | Ads that lead to a sale on the same platform | Alibaba and ByteDance; Tencent building |
| Licences and compliance | Game licences, payment licences, content approvals | Incumbents with long regulatory records |
| Compute and models | Access to chips, model quality, inference cost | Alibaba (own chips, cloud scale), ByteDance, Tencent |
| Capital | Ability to fund losses in new products | All the large groups; ByteDance and Tencent generate the most profit |
| Price | Take rates in payments, ad prices, cloud prices, token prices | Matters most in cloud and AI APIs |

### Structural Dynamics and Outlook

The structure has been stable for a decade: Tencent owns communication, Alibaba owns commerce, and ByteDance took most of the growth in user time through short video. The contested territory is where these meet — short video (Video Accounts against Douyin and Kuaishou), closed-loop advertising (Mini Shops against Taobao and Douyin), and now AI agents, where an assistant that books, buys and pays on the user's behalf could reroute traffic between platforms. Scale advantages compound in AI because training cost is fixed and inference cost falls with volume, which is why the largest groups are each spending tens of billions of renminbi a quarter [2][38]. The risks to incumbents are AI interfaces that bypass existing apps, regulation (which has changed the economics of games and fintech before), and chip access.

In summary: Tencent's core markets — messaging, games and payments — are concentrated and protected by network effects and licences; its growth markets — advertising, cloud and AI — are contested by rivals of similar size, and the AI race is raising capital intensity across the industry.

**What moved margins.**

### What the reader must understand about the financials

**1. Two sets of profit numbers, and the gap is widening.** From 2026 the company invites readers to look at operating profit "excluding new AI products". That figure grew 17%, 19% and 8% in the first three quarters of 2026; reported non-IFRS operating profit grew 9%, 9% and −6% [2][5][7]. The difference — the derived AI-products drag of RMB 34.5bn over nine months — is a management-defined carve-out; the allocation of shared infrastructure and staff between "core" and "new AI products" is not audited or explained in the documents read.

**2. Capital intensity has changed.** Accrual capex was RMB 23.9bn in 2023, under 4% of revenue [9]. In the first nine months of 2026 it was RMB 166.1bn, 27% of revenue [2][5][7]. Management describes cash capex of about RMB 65bn a quarter as the peak, holding through 2027 [3]. On top of capex, Bernstein reports a further RMB 86bn of compute prepayments booked in Q3 2026 [41]. Depreciation follows with a lag: depreciation of PP&E, investment properties and right-of-use assets was RMB 32.8bn in all of 2025 and RMB 11.2bn in Q2 2026 alone [1][4].

**3. The investment portfolio is large relative to the operating business and moves IFRS earnings.** Listed and unlisted stakes totalled RMB 902.6bn at September 2026 [2]. Fair-value swings and disposals pass through IFRS profit and the share of associates, which is why Tencent and most analysts use non-IFRS figures.

**No long-term algorithm.** Tencent has never published a medium-term revenue or margin target, payout ratio or leverage target. The closest thing to a framework is the "two businesses" view and the "moats" list given by Martin Lau [6][8].

**Strategic investment programme.** The AI programme has four parts as management describes it: foundation models (Hy3 in production, Hy4 released November 2026, Hy5 planned), productivity agents (WorkBuddy, CodeBuddy), consumer gateways (Yuanbao, Xiaowei), and the compute to run them, part of which is rented out through Tencent Cloud [2][3][6]. Management says that if the applications fail to earn a return, the compute can be rented out "at cost recovery or even better prices" [6]. AI is also expected to change how the core is run: management said existing businesses could operate with fewer people and redeploy staff, at a "very early stage" [3].

**Leadership.** No changes to the executive team or board leadership have been announced in the documents read.

### Key risks to the outlook

**AI spending duration.** Management's case depends on model-training compute being a two-year "lump sum". Its own November 2026 statement puts peak cash capex through 2027 [3], and the AI-products drag has grown every quarter of 2026 (RMB 8.8bn → 10.5bn → 15.2bn, derived) [2][5][7]. Management said the timing of payback is unknown [3].

**Depreciation catching up with earnings.** Nine-month 2026 capex of RMB 166.1bn compares with full-year 2025 depreciation and amortisation of RMB 66.0bn [1][2]. As the 2026–27 spending is depreciated, reported margins carry the cost whether or not the related revenue arrives.

**Consumer and advertising weakness.** Payments value per transaction has been falling, the ad market has been weak "for several months", and e-commerce platforms are shifting merchant budgets to subsidies [3][6][8]. Advertising is Tencent's fastest-growing large line.

**Chip access.** US rules restrict advanced GPUs and, since 2026, remote access to them. Tencent says inference is unaffected but training will be slower, and it can no longer offer high-end GPUs to cloud customers [3].

**Regulation of games and fintech.** The December 2023 draft game rules showed how quickly the regulator can move [18]; approvals are currently at multi-year highs [26]. Fintech oversight was extended in late 2025 [33].

**Competition for time from short video and AI apps.** Douyin holds 44% of mobile video time and Doubao leads consumer AI apps by a wide margin [29][30]; Tencent's Video Accounts time per DAU is less than half the leaders' [3].
