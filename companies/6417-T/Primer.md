# 1. Company Overview

**Source limitations.** All company figures come from SANKYO's own filings and IR documents: the FY3/2026 securities report (yuho), the FY3/2026 earnings release, and the Q1 and H1 FY3/2027 results presentations [1][2][4][5]. The yuho text was read through its notes, but the major-customer note and the consolidated balance-sheet detail were only partly extracted; where a parent-only figure is used instead, the text says so. ## 1.1 SANKYO (6417.T) — Company Primer

### Company Overview

SANKYO designs and sells pachinko and pachislot machines, the two kinds of gaming machine installed in Japan's roughly 6,500 licensed pachinko halls, plus the ball-supply and card systems those halls use [1][12]. The company is listed on the Prime Market of the Tokyo Stock Exchange (ticker 6417). It is headquartered in Shibuya, Tokyo, and was founded in Nagoya in April 1966 [1]. The representative director and president is Toshio Ogura. The chairman is Hideyuki Busujima, who ran the company as president from 1996 and as CEO from 2008 [1]. In the yuho the legal name is 株式会社三共; the trading name is SANKYO, and its flagship pachinko series is branded "Fever" [1].

In FY3/2026 SANKYO had consolidated sales of ¥179.2bn, operating profit of ¥62.5bn (a 34.9% margin) and net profit of ¥46.8bn [2]. It sold about 251,600 pachinko machines and 90,600 pachislot machines [5] and had 895 consolidated employees [1]. Equity was 86.5% of total assets and there was no interest-bearing debt [1]. The business is a design house with in-house final assembly. It develops a stream of titles (most tied to licensed anime, manga or film content), gets each one type-approved under the Adult Entertainment Act regime, and sells the machines in short runs to hall operators [1]. Each title earns most of its revenue in the weeks around launch.

Management's stated aim is to remain the leader in both pachinko and pachislot through creative product development, while cutting cost by standardising parts and reusing components [1]. The current medium-term plan (FY3/2025–FY3/2027) adds two goals: build a "solid pachinko business structure" and "accelerate pachislot growth". It targets return on equity (ROE) of 15–20% [7]. In FY3/2027 the company added two initiatives aimed at the shrinking pachinko player base: a pricing policy called "SANKYO Yell Price" that lowers the cost of new machines to halls, and a player-acquisition programme called KUGITAMA [1][3].

### Segments

SANKYO reports three operating segments plus "other": pachinko machines, pachislot machines, and ball/token supply equipment (補給機器) [1].

| Pachinko machines | 119.8 | 66.9% | 49.4 | 41.2% |
| Pachislot machines | 43.4 | 24.2% | 18.9 | 43.6% |
| Supply equipment | 15.5 | 8.7% | 1.1 | 7.1% |
| Other (rental, molded parts) | 0.4 | 0.2% | 0.2 | 44.9% |
| Consolidated (after corporate costs) | 179.2 | 100% | 62.5 | 34.9% |

*Sources: [1][2]. Segment profit is before unallocated corporate costs of ¥7.1bn [1].*

**Pachinko machines.** Machines sold under the SANKYO, Bisty and JB brands, together with reused cabinets, gauge boards and parts, plus royalties received through the industry patent pool [1]. Pachinko is the larger and more stable segment by volume: SANKYO has held more than 30% of pachinko unit sales for four consecutive years [3]. Segment sales rose 11.2% in FY3/2026 on higher unit volume [1].

**Pachislot machines.** Pachislot (slot-type) machines under the SANKYO and Bisty brands [1]. This segment is more volatile because it depends heavily on the timing of type-approval for new titles. In FY3/2026 sales fell 31.6% and segment profit fell 47%. Three planned titles were pushed back by approval delays, so SANKYO launched four new titles against six a year earlier [1][3].

**Supply equipment.** Hall equipment: ball and medal supply systems, card units and peripherals. This is a low-margin business (7.1%) that tracks hall capex cycles [1]. Sales fell 22.9% in FY3/2026 after the 2024 banknote redesign brought forward hall spending on cash-handling equipment (inference from the timing; supplier Daikoku Denki describes the same effect) [1][14].

**Other.** Building rental and general molded parts; immaterial [1].

There have been no segment realignments in the last two years. However, the medium-term plan measures pachinko units for "SANKYO + JB" and Bisty separately, and the H1 FY3/2027 presentation splits units by brand and by machine type: frame-type (枠) sales, meaning complete cabinets, and gauge-type (ゲージ盤) sales, meaning replacement game boards fitted into an existing cabinet [5][7]. That brand and type mix moves average revenue per unit and should be watched in any period comparison.

### How the Business Model Works

**Where revenue comes from, mechanically.** SANKYO sells each machine outright to a hall operator, on credit terms. Revenue is recognised when the hall inspects and accepts the machine [1]. A title is a product: one game design built around a licensed content property (for example *Evangelion*, *Gundam*, *Tokyo Ghoul*), engineered to a specific payout specification, approved as a "type" (型式) by the designated testing body, and then manufactured in a run that usually lasts weeks rather than months [1][11]. Halls buy in two ways. They buy a complete new cabinet (frame-type, the full machine), or, for pachinko, a replacement game board and display (gauge-type) that fits into a SANKYO cabinet they already own. The second is cheaper for the hall because the cabinet is reused; rival Heiwa describes gauge-board sales as high-margin, and SANKYO does not split margin by type [5][22]. The company also sells reused machines. Their revenue is recognised net of the cost of collecting trade-ins, and expected returns on machines sold with return rights are deducted from revenue [1]. A smaller stream is royalty income from the patent pool, recognised when licensees report their sales [1].

Revenue per unit can be approximated from the segment figures. Pachinko segment sales of ¥119.8bn on about 251,600 units implies roughly ¥476,000 per unit including parts, reuse and royalties. Pachislot sales of ¥43.4bn on 90,600 units implies roughly ¥480,000 per unit [1][5]. These are calculations, not disclosed prices. The company does not publish list prices, and the new "Yell Price" policy deliberately lowers the SANKYO-brand price per unit in FY3/2027 [3].

**Cost structure and unit economics.** In H1 FY3/2027 gross margin was 59.6% and SG&A was ¥21.0bn, giving an operating margin of 33.7% [5]. Cost of goods is mainly electronic components (including semiconductors and LCD panels), resin and mechanical parts. The company names Kaga Electronics as a parts supplier and identifies semiconductors and petroleum-based resins as procurement risks [1]. The largest fixed cost is development: R&D was ¥21.6bn in FY3/2026 (12.0% of sales; ¥14.8bn for pachinko and ¥6.7bn for pachislot), carried by 280 development staff [1]. Licence fees for the content in each title are a further cost that the filings do not quantify. Because development is fixed and each title's tooling is modest (pachinko mold and tooling capex was ¥2.4bn in FY3/2026), margin depends heavily on units per title [1]. A hit that runs to a second or third production batch (増産) carries far higher incremental margin than the first batch. The swing in segment profit shows this: pachislot sales fell 31.6% in FY3/2026 but segment profit fell 47.0% [1].

**The demand engine.** Machine demand is a replacement market, not a growth market. Halls replace machines to keep their floor attractive to players, and the number of new titles a hall buys depends on its gross gaming revenue ("utilisation") and its cash flow [3][12]. At the end of 2025 there were about 1.87 million pachinko machines and 1.36 million pachislot machines installed in halls, according to National Police Agency data reported in the press [13]. Annual new-machine sales of about 0.80 million pachinko and 0.73 million pachislot units in FY3/2026 imply that halls replace roughly 40–55% of the installed base each year [5][13] (inference). Three things therefore drive volume:

1. **Player activity and spend**, which sets hall revenue and appetite for new machines. The Japan Productivity Center estimated 6.9 million pachinko players in 2024, up 0.3 million from the record low in 2023 [15].
2. **Hall count and floor size.** Halls keep closing, at about 3.8% a year in 2025, but the survivors are larger [12].
3. **Regulation and type-approval throughput**, which decides how many new specifications can reach the market and when [1][19].

Demand is not split by the company into recurring and cyclical parts. In practice the base is the steady replacement of an installed base of over three million machines. On top of that sit the hit cycle (which titles succeed) and a regulatory cycle (new machine standards force or enable waves of replacement) [11][18].

**The reinforcing structure.** SANKYO's share leadership in pachinko feeds itself in three ways.

- *Installed cabinets.* Every SANKYO frame-type sale puts a cabinet on a hall floor that can later take a cheaper SANKYO gauge-type board. The H1 FY3/2027 presentation shows 35,574 of SANKYO's 61,997 brand pachinko units were gauge-type [5]. This gives SANKYO a captive replacement channel for its own installed base (inference from the frame and gauge disclosure).
- *Multiple brands.* The group sells under SANKYO, Bisty, JB and, since September 2026, A-gon. Halls cap how many machines they buy from any one brand in a refresh, so separate brands let the group place more titles per cycle [5].
- *Development scale.* The company spends more than ¥20bn a year on R&D and leads the industry in patent citations against rivals [1][25]. This supports a broad title pipeline: 11 pachinko and 8 pachislot titles are planned for FY3/2027 [3].

None of this creates contractual recurring revenue. Each title must still sell on its merits.

**Go-to-market.** SANKYO sells directly to halls through its own sales branches and offices. Bisty sells pachinko and pachislot machines through distribution contracts with Fields Corporation (now Tsuburaya Fields Holdings), which the yuho lists as a material contract renewed annually [1]. The yuho reports no customer above 10% of consolidated sales in FY3/2025 or FY3/2026 [1]. The KUGITAMA initiative adds a new transaction model: from November 2026, *P Hane BASTARD!!* is offered to halls under a rental plan starting at ¥20,000 per machine per month before tax, instead of an outright sale [5]. The filings do not yet show whether rental will become material.

**Key operating metrics.** Management reports units sold by segment and brand, the number of new titles, market unit estimates for pachinko and pachislot, and SANKYO's unit share. The share targets are above 30% in pachinko and 15% or more in pachislot [3][7]. It also reports operating margin and ROE against its plan targets [1].

**Capital model.** The balance sheet is almost all cash and working capital. Cash and equivalents were ¥192.4bn at March 2026 against total assets of ¥287.5bn [1]. Surplus cash has been returned through buybacks: ¥60bn in FY3/2026 and 30 million shares cancelled [1]. In August 2026 the dividend policy changed from a 40% payout to the higher of a 50% payout or a dividend on equity (DOE) of 5% [4]. Part 4 covers capital allocation.

Recent titles carry the industry's current specifications. In pachinko, "smart pachinko" (スマパチ, ball-less play counted electronically) has been on sale since April 2023, and the "Lucky Trigger" (LT) specification since March 2024, with an "LT3.0 Plus" variant since July 2025 [11][14]. ### Corporate History

SANKYO was incorporated in Nagoya in April 1966. It opened a factory in Kiryu, Gunma in 1975 and moved its headquarters there in 1981 [1]. The "Fever" pachinko line launched in 1980 and became the brand that defined the company [1]. The structurally consequential events since then are:

- **1992–1996: building the group.** SANKYO acquired the molding company that became SANKYO EXCEL, and in 1996 acquired Daido, now Bisty. This created the second machine brand and the Fields distribution channel [1].
- **1995–1997: listing.** Listed on the TSE Second Section in 1995 and moved to the First Section in 1997 [1].
- **2001: Sanwa plant** in Isesaki, Gunma, still the main assembly site [1].
- **2008 peak and the long decline.** Consolidated sales peaked at about ¥280.5bn in FY3/2008, then fell sharply three times: by 33% in FY3/2009, 40% in FY3/2013 and 41% in FY3/2017. They reached a low of ¥58.1bn in FY3/2021 during COVID [24][5].
- **2012: JB acquired** [1].
- **2022–2024: recovery and capital restructuring.** Sales rebounded to ¥199.1bn in FY3/2024 as smart machines and new specifications lifted demand [5]. A 5-for-1 stock split took effect on 1 March 2024, and the company adopted an audit-and-supervisory-committee board structure in June 2024 [1].
- **2025–2026: capital return and HQ purchase.** In FY3/2026 SANKYO bought back ¥60bn of shares and cancelled 30 million shares. In September 2025 it bought its Shibuya headquarters for ¥12.95bn from Murf Corporation, a company wholly owned by the chairman [1]. In August 2026 it raised the dividend payout policy, and in September 2026 it acquired A-gon [4][5].

The history in the filings is thin before the 1990s, and the yuho gives no reason for the three revenue collapses. Part 3 explains them as regulatory cycles.

# 2. Company Logistics

## 2.1 Customers and Distribution

**Who the customers are**

SANKYO's customers are pachinko hall operators. In 2025 Japan had about 6,450 licensed halls, down 256 (3.8%) from a year earlier, according to Yano Research [12]. The customer base runs from national chains with hundreds of very large halls to single-site family businesses. Yano notes that closures are concentrated in halls with 300 machines or fewer and, increasingly, in the 301–500 machine bracket. Halls with more than 1,000 machines have become common, and the large chains are taking share by offering a wider choice of popular titles [12].

SANKYO does not disclose sales by customer or by customer tier. The yuho states that no single customer accounted for 10% or more of consolidated sales in either FY3/2025 or FY3/2026 [1]. The only related disclosure is that Bisty sells through Fields Corporation under distribution contracts [1]. Customer concentration is therefore low at the hall level. Exposure is instead concentrated in the hall industry's collective willingness to buy.

Hall purchasing behaviour is the variable that matters, and the company comments on it every quarter:

| Period | Pachinko market (company view) | Pachislot market (company view) |
|---|---|---|
| FY3/2026 | LT3.0 Plus spread smart pachinko but did not revive the market; weak utilisation cut hall investment appetite | Solid utilisation; units flat because low type-approval pass rates restricted supply |
| Q1 FY3/2027 | Halls "even more cautious" in choosing new machines; market about 190,000 units, −12% | Stable demand; market about 180,000 units, +2% |
| H1 FY3/2027 | About 370,000 units, below the prior year; utilisation not improving | About 350,000 units, solid; approval pass rate still low |

*Sources: [3][4][6][5].*

The pattern is that halls are moving their capex from pachinko to pachislot. SANKYO's H1 FY3/2027 numbers show this directly: pachinko units fell 34.4% while pachislot units rose 12.7% [5].

**Distribution channels**

SANKYO sells through two routes [1]:

1. **Direct sales by the SANKYO brand** through its own branch and sales-office network. These branches, alongside the Shibuya headquarters and the Sanwa plant, are listed as main facilities in the yuho [1].
2. **Bisty-brand machines through Fields.** Bisty's contracts with Fields cover both pachinko and pachislot machines and renew annually [1].

JB and the newly acquired A-gon sell through their own channels; the filings do not give their channel arrangements [1][5].

The filings disclose no channel KPIs (orders per branch, share through Fields). The yuho does give an order book. FY3/2026 orders received were ¥161.3bn (−21.9%), and the order backlog at March 2026 was ¥17.7bn (−49.7%) [1].

| Segment | Orders received FY3/2026 (¥bn) | Backlog at Mar 2026 (¥bn) |
|---|---|---|
| Pachinko | 106.6 | 12.2 |
| Pachislot | 39.1 | 4.6 |
| Supply equipment | 15.6 | 0.9 |
| Total | 161.3 | 17.7 |

*Source: [1].*

The backlog covers less than six weeks of annual sales. This is consistent with a model where halls order titles shortly before launch rather than placing long-dated orders (inference).

**Transaction models in transition.** Two changes affect how revenue is earned:

- **SANKYO Yell Price.** In FY3/2027 SANKYO lowered prices on SANKYO-brand pachinko machines. It argues that component costs and design complexity had pushed machine prices too high for halls to earn a return [3]. Management calls this a growth investment that should raise utilisation, market volume and share, and says it expects competitors to follow [3]. It lowers SANKYO-brand revenue per unit in FY3/2027; the company has not disclosed the size of the cut [3].
- **Rental.** From November 2026, *P Hane BASTARD!! -Darkness Destroyer-* is offered under the KUGITAMA YELL rental plan from ¥20,000 per unit per month before tax [5]. Rental converts a one-off sale into recurring income. Accounting treatment and scale are not yet disclosed.

## 2.2 Suppliers and Procurement

SANKYO makes its machines itself. Final assembly is at the Sanwa plant in Isesaki, Gunma, and molded resin and electronic parts come from subsidiary SANKYO EXCEL in Midori, Gunma [1]. The yuho does not describe outsourced contract manufacturing [1].

Key purchased inputs are electronic components, including semiconductors and LCD displays, petroleum-based resins, and mechanical parts [1]. The yuho names three counterparties:

- **Kaga Electronics**, a supplier of pachinko and pachislot parts [1].
- **Daikoku Denki**, a supplier of supply equipment [1].
- **Game Card Holdings**, a supply-equipment counterparty in which SANKYO holds 1.40 million shares as a strategic stake [1].

SANKYO does not disclose purchase shares by supplier or cost sensitivities. Its procurement risks are listed in the yuho: semiconductor and resin shortages and price rises, including from Middle East tensions. Its mitigations are early ordering, multiple suppliers and holding inventory [1]. Inventory reflects that last point. Raw materials and supplies at the parent were ¥10.7bn at March 2026, ten times work-in-process [1]. The yuho also shows a receivable for paid supply of parts to contractors (有償支給未収入金) of ¥5.6bn at March 2026 (¥12.2bn a year earlier). This indicates that SANKYO buys some components centrally and supplies them to sub-assemblers. The notes do not explain the arrangement [1].

Content licences are the other significant input. Most titles are built on third-party anime, manga or film properties. The yuho names rising disputes over character and celebrity rights as a risk but does not quantify licence costs [1]. The group is also building its own content IP (manga, anime) to reduce dependence on licensors [1][7].

There is no tariff exposure on sales, because sales are entirely domestic (see 2.3). Component costs carry indirect currency and trade exposure; the company says rising component costs are one reason for its pricing review [3].

## 2.3 Geographic Distribution

SANKYO sells only in Japan. Pachinko and pachislot machines are type-approved under Japanese law for Japanese halls, and the company reports no overseas sales or overseas segment [1]. The yuho's segment information is not split by region [1].

Within Japan the filings give no regional sales split. The best available proxy is the installed base by prefecture, which is published from National Police Agency data by trade press (secondary; not compiled here) [13]. At the national level the installed base has shrunk steadily:

|---|---|---|
| 2021 | 2.34 | 1.48 |
| 2022 | 2.21 | 1.36 |
| 2023 | 2.08 | 1.35 |
| 2024 | 1.97 | 1.36 |

The pachinko installed base fell by about 20% in four years, while the pachislot base stabilised from 2023 and rose slightly in 2024 and 2025 [13]. Yano Research forecasts 5,600 halls and 3.08 million installed machines in 2030 [12].

Because the market is purely domestic, SANKYO has no direct foreign-exchange exposure on sales. The yuho describes its only currency effects as cost-side, through imported components [1].

# 3. Industry Overview

## 3.1 Industry Overview

**Introduction**

Pachinko is a Japanese gaming format played in licensed halls. Players rent steel balls and launch them into a vertical board studded with pins (釘, "nails"). Balls that fall into winning pockets trigger a digital lottery shown on a central LCD, and a win pays out more balls. Pachislot is a slot-machine format played with medals or, in today's "smart" version, electronic credits [11][14]. Halls are regulated under the Act on Control and Improvement of Amusement Business (風営法, the Adult Entertainment Act). Each machine design must pass a type test by a designated testing body before it can be sold, and payout performance is capped by regulation [1][18].

The industry has three layers. Machine makers such as SANKYO design and sell machines. Hall operators buy machines, run the halls and collect players' spending. Peripheral suppliers sell ball and medal handling, card and data systems [11]. Machine makers depend on hall operators' gross gaming revenue and willingness to keep refreshing their floors. Halls in turn depend on new titles to keep players coming back, so new machines are a recurring cost of staying competitive for the hall (inference from the replacement rates in Part 1).

**Industry size**

There is no single agreed measure of the industry. The widely quoted "market size" is gross player spending (balls rented), not hall revenue or machine sales, and it is far larger than the machine market.

| Estimate | Year | Source | Scope / definition |
|---|---|---|---|
| ¥16.2tn | 2024 | Japan Productivity Center, Leisure White Paper 2025 | Gross player spending on pachinko and pachislot [15] |
| ¥14.6tn | 2020–2021 | Leisure White Paper 2022, cited in an industry report | Same measure; COVID low [17] |
| ¥861.3bn | FY2024 | Yano Research | Machine makers' sales of pachinko-related equipment: pachinko machines ¥380.4bn, pachislot ¥310.4bn, peripherals ¥170.5bn [11] |
| 870,000 pachinko and 658,000 pachislot units | Calendar 2025 | Pachinko Village | New-machine unit deliveries, independent survey [10] |
| 799,000 pachinko and 729,000 pachislot units | FY3/2026 (Apr–Mar) | SANKYO estimate | New-machine unit sales, company estimate [5] |

The gross-spending figures overstate the economic size of the business. A large share of balls rented is won back by players during play; halls keep only the net. The machine-market figure is the one that matters for a machine maker. On Yano's numbers it shrank 2.4% in FY2024: pachinko machines grew 4.8% and pachislot fell 11.2% [11].

The two unit series differ because of period and method. Pachinko Village counts calendar-year deliveries and rounds to 500 units [10]. SANKYO estimates the market for its own fiscal year [5]. Both show the same direction: pachinko units recovered in 2025 and then fell again in 2026, while pachislot held at roughly 0.7 million.

The long-run trend is decline. On the Leisure White Paper measure, player spending fell from more than ¥30tn in the mid-2000s to ¥14.6tn in 2020–21 [17]. Hall numbers keep falling, to about 6,450 in 2025, and Yano forecasts 5,600 halls and 3.08 million installed machines by 2030 [12]. The player base shrank to 6.9 million in 2024, slightly up from a record low the year before [15].

**How the industry works**

*Value chain.* Machine makers own the design: the game, the content licence, the payout specification and the cabinet. They buy components (LCD panels, semiconductors, resin, mechanisms), assemble in Japan, and submit each design to a type test [1][19]. Approved designs are sold to halls, which carry the inventory and install the machines. Peripheral suppliers (Daikoku Denki, Game Card Holdings) sell the medal and ball handling, card and data units that sit next to each machine [1][14].

*Smart machines changed the hall's equipment.* Smart pachislot (medal-less, from November 2022) and smart pachinko (ball-less, from April 2023) require a dedicated hall unit. Once that unit is installed, a hall can switch between smart pachislot and smart pachinko quickly. Yano argues this lets halls move capex between the two formats faster than before [11][14]. Daikoku Denki reports that smart pachislot reached 61.1% of installed pachislot machines and about 87.7% of pachislot units sold in FY3/2026. Smart pachinko reached 28.5% of installed pachinko machines and about 63.9% of pachinko units sold [14].

*Pricing and contracts.* Machines are sold outright, title by title. There is no subscription or revenue-share model in the mainstream market. SANKYO's KUGITAMA rental plan is an experiment at the low-cost end [1][5]. Yano describes a trend toward "small lots", meaning fewer units per title: even 10,000 units is now a high bar for a pachinko model. The top 10 pachinko models averaged about 26,000 units in FY2024, down from about 29,000 in FY2023 [11].

*Business models and margins.* Makers that dominate the replacement cycle earn very high margins. SANKYO's pachinko segment earned 41.2% in FY3/2026 [1], while Heiwa's machine segment earned about 26% in FY3/2025 (¥11.9bn on ¥45.5bn sales) [22]. Margins are high because a successful title is sold in volume after development is paid for. They fall sharply when titles miss (inference from the segment volatility shown in Part 1).

**Demand drivers and cyclicality**

Demand for new machines follows four things:

1. **Player participation and spend**, which sets hall revenue. The player base fell to a record low in 2023 and recovered slightly in 2024 [15]. The 2026 Leisure White Paper reports a 2025 leisure market of ¥78.1tn, up 3.9% year on year. The press coverage reviewed does not give the 2025 pachinko participant figure [16].
2. **Hall consolidation**, which removes floor space but concentrates purchasing in larger halls [12].
3. **Regulatory cycles.** A new machine standard (a "No." generation) forces halls to replace machines that no longer comply. A relaxation of internal rules makes new specifications possible and stimulates buying [18][29].
4. **Equipment cycles at halls.** The 2024 banknote redesign forced spending on cash-handling equipment, and smart machines forced spending on dedicated units [11][14].

Type-approval capacity is a hard constraint on supply. SANKYO launched four new pachislot titles in FY3/2026 against a plan that had three more, because those three missed approval [3]. The approval bottleneck also explains why pachislot units held up even as halls preferred pachislot: supply, not demand, limited the market [3].

**Key industry trends and developments**

*Pachislot over pachinko.* Halls are moving their machine budgets toward pachislot. Utilisation of pachislot is described as solid, while pachinko utilisation has not responded to new specifications [3][6]. Yano notes halls tend to buy new pachislot models immediately on release [11]. The installed base shows the shift: pachinko machines fell from 2.34 million to 1.87 million between 2021 and 2025, while pachislot stabilised at about 1.36 million [13].

*Smart machines.* The changeover to medal-less and ball-less machines is largely complete for pachislot sales (87.7% of units) and about two-thirds done for pachinko sales [14]. Once halls have the dedicated units, the format choice becomes more fluid [11].

*Bigger halls, fewer halls.* Closures are concentrated in small and mid-sized halls; survivors are larger [12]. Purchasing power is concentrating in large operators, but no hall operator accounts for 10% of SANKYO's sales [1].

*Fewer units per title.* Average sales per hit model are falling. Halls spread spending across more titles and buy in smaller lots [11]. This rewards makers with a broad pipeline and penalises one-hit makers (inference).

*Price pressure on machines.* Machine prices rose with component costs and complexity. SANKYO's own Yell Price policy is an admission that halls struggle to earn a return on new machines at current prices [3].

*Player base.* The core pachinko population is shrinking and ageing. SANKYO's KUGITAMA programme, which targets dormant and new players with simulators, events and low-cost rentals, is a direct response [1][5]. The filings acknowledge "signs of stabilisation and some younger players, but no clear growth yet" [1].

**Competitive landscape**

The machine market is concentrated at the top in pachinko and fragmented in pachislot. Most of the large makers are private.

SANKYO's own figures agree with the pachinko position: it reports more than 30% of pachinko units for a fourth consecutive year in FY3/2026 [3]. Yano says SANKYO's pachinko sales are more than double the runner-up's, and that in FY2024 SANKYO rose to first place in pachislot by both units and value [11]. The pachislot share is less stable. On SANKYO's own fiscal numbers it was about 12% in FY3/2026 (90,600 units against a 729,000 market) and about 13% in H1 FY3/2027 (46,100 against 350,000). These are calculations from [5].

**Level of competitive intensity**

Intensity is **moderate in pachinko and high in pachislot**.

In pachinko, SANKYO's scale, broad title pipeline and installed cabinet base give it a structural lead. The second-largest group sells about a third of SANKYO's volume [10]. Competition is about which titles succeed in a given quarter rather than about price, because halls buy a title for the play it generates, not for a discount (inference). SANKYO's Yell Price policy is a deliberate move to use price as a lever in a shrinking market, which may raise intensity if rivals follow, as management expects [3].

In pachislot, share swings with hits and approvals. Kitadenshi's *Juggler* series sold 75,000 units from one model in 2025. Universal, Sammy and Enterrise each held between 8% and 13% [10]. A maker can rise or fall several places in a year depending on approval timing. SANKYO's own pachislot unit sales fell from 131,000 in FY3/2025 to 91,000 in FY3/2026 on approval delays [4].

Barriers to entry are high. They include development cost (SANKYO spends more than ¥20bn a year), the type-test process, hall relationships, content licensing and patents: SANKYO ranked first in patent citations against rivals in 2025 [1][25]. Barriers to scale are lower in pachislot, where a single hit can carry a small maker [10].

**Basis of competition**

| Dimension | What it means in this industry | Who it favours |
|---|---|---|
| Title quality (play and payout spec) | Whether a machine keeps players and earns its hall a return | Makers with deep development teams; hit-driven |
| Content licences | Popular anime, manga and film IP draws players | Makers able to pay for and secure top IP; SANKYO building its own IP [1][7] |
| Approval execution | Getting designs through type tests on schedule | Makers with large R&D and testing experience; low pass rates hit everyone [19] |
| Pipeline breadth | Many titles a year across specifications and price points | Large groups with multiple brands (SANKYO's four brands) [5] |
| Installed cabinet base | Ability to sell cheaper replacement boards into own cabinets | Market leaders in pachinko [5] |
| Price and hall economics | Machine cost versus the revenue it earns for the hall | Low-cost makers; SANKYO's Yell Price targets this directly [3] |
| Distribution | Reach into halls, directly or through distributors such as Fields | Established makers [1] |

**Structural dynamics and outlook**

The industry is in long-term contraction. Halls, installed machines and players are all falling, and Yano expects the decline to continue at a slower pace to 2030 [12][15]. Within that contraction, value is moving toward pachislot, toward larger halls, and toward makers with broad pipelines that can keep placing titles as lots shrink [11][13].

Consolidation among makers is visible at the edges. SANKYO acquired A-gon in September 2026 [5]. Heiwa now earns most of its profit from golf [21][22]. Sega Sammy's machine segment remains its most profitable business even as the group invests elsewhere [20][26]. Regulation is both the main risk and the main stimulus. Each new specification (LT, BT, the "normal-time flag" design) is an attempt to revive play within the payout caps, and type-approval throughput sets the pace of supply [6][11][19].

In summary, the machine market is a shrinking replacement market of roughly ¥700bn a year in machine sales. Demand depends on hall economics and regulatory specifications, and the leaders hold their position through pipeline breadth, content and approval execution rather than price [11]. SANKYO is the clear leader in pachinko and a top-tier but volatile player in pachislot.

The medium-term plan assumed both machine markets would grow on smart-machine adoption, with pachislot outselling pachinko from 2024 [7]. Actual pachinko demand fell instead, and management attributes the shortfall to market conditions [3]. A new medium-term plan is due after FY3/2027, the final year of the current plan; none has been announced [7].

**Strategic programmes.**
- **SANKYO Yell Price:** lower SANKYO-brand pachinko prices to restore halls' returns on machines [3].
- **KUGITAMA:** player acquisition through simulators, events, the online museum and a rental plan from ¥20,000 per unit per month [5][6].
- **A-gon:** a fourth brand to broaden the lineup [5].
- **Content IP:** own manga and anime to reduce licensing dependence [1][7].
- **R&D:** planned at ¥22.8bn in FY3/2027 [5].

**Leadership.** Toshio Ogura is representative director and president. Hideyuki Busujima, president from 1996 and CEO from 2008, is chairman and holds 4.0% directly, with family members holding further stakes [1]. No succession change has been announced.

**Key risks to the outlook**

*Pachinko demand.* Pachinko market units are forecast at 700,000 for FY3/2027, down from 1,055,000 in FY3/2023 [5]. The installed base fell 20% between 2021 and 2025 [13]. Management says new specifications have not yet revived utilisation [6]. Pachinko is still 62% of H1 sales [5].

*Type-approval throughput.* Three pachislot titles slipped out of FY3/2026 on approval delays [3]. Pachislot pass rates were around 10% in early 2025 [19]. The FY3/2027 plan needs about 81,000 pachislot units in H2 from five new titles [5].

*Price policy.* Yell Price lowers revenue per SANKYO-brand pachinko machine by an undisclosed amount. Management describes it as a growth investment that depends on halls' utilisation recovering and on volume rising [3]. If volume does not respond, the margin falls with no offset (inference from the stated mechanism).

*Hit concentration.* Two titles each sold more than 25,000 units in H1 FY3/2027 [5]. Yano reports that units per title are falling across the industry [11]. A missed title cannot easily be replaced within the year, because development takes one to two years [1].

*Content and IP disputes.* The yuho identifies rising disputes over character and celebrity rights as a risk to machines built on licensed content [1].

*Governance and related parties.* The chairman's wholly owned company Murf Corporation leased office space to SANKYO and sold it the headquarters for ¥12.95bn in 2025. The board approved the deal without the related directors, at a price based on independent appraisals [1]. Murf also holds 2.01% of SANKYO's shares [1].
