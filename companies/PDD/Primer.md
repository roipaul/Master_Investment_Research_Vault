## 1.1 PDD Holdings Inc. (PDD) — Company Primer

### Company Overview

PDD Holdings runs two online marketplaces that sell low-priced goods made mostly by Chinese manufacturers and farms. **Pinduoduo** serves consumers in China. **Temu**, launched in September 2022, serves consumers in the United States, Europe, Japan and dozens of other countries [1]. The company's American depositary shares (ADS) trade on Nasdaq under the ticker PDD; one ADS represents four Class A ordinary shares. The listed parent is a Cayman Islands holding company with its principal executive office in Dublin, Ireland. Its operations in mainland China run through subsidiaries and a consolidated variable interest entity (VIE) [1]. Since December 2025 the company has been led by two co-chairmen who are also co-chief executives. Lei Chen, a founding member, was CEO from July 2020 and chairman from March 2021. Jiazhen Zhao, also a founding member, built the Duo Duo Grocery business and became co-CEO in April 2023 [1]. Founder Zheng (Colin) Huang holds no executive title but remains the largest shareholder, with 24.8% of ordinary shares through affiliated entities [1].

Four numbers define the scale of the business. In FY2025 revenue was RMB 431.8bn (US$61.8bn), up 9.7% [1]. Net income was RMB 97.8bn, down 13% from RMB 112.4bn in FY2024 [1]. The platforms had 16.8 million active merchants, against 15.8 million in FY2024 [1]. The company employed only 25,474 people at the end of 2025. That is roughly RMB 17m of revenue per employee, low staffing for a business of this size [1]. Substantially all revenue comes from merchants based in China, including the revenue Temu earns from overseas consumers [1].

The core model is an **asset-light, third-party marketplace** that monetises merchants, not consumers. PDD does not own the goods sold on Pinduoduo, does not run its own delivery fleet, and books revenue as an agent. It records the fees and advertising it earns from merchants, not the value of the goods [1]. Merchants pay in two ways: by bidding for placement in the app's search and recommendation feeds ("online marketing services") and through commissions and other transaction-linked fees ("transaction services") [1]. Temu uses the same agent accounting but has historically taken on more of the selling: in its original "fully managed" model, Temu set prices, ran marketing and arranged cross-border delivery for merchants who shipped their goods to Temu's warehouses in China [11]. Since 2025 the company has also funded large merchant-support programmes. In 2026 it began building a first-party brand business, its first move away from a pure marketplace [4][5].

Management frames the current period in its own words as the start of a "new decade of high-quality development" [3]. Its stated priorities are supply-chain investment ("the strongest commitment", in Jiazhen Zhao's FY2025 results statement), merchant support, compliance and governance, and adapting the global business to changing trade rules [6][3]. The financial team describes the company as being in a "strategic investment phase" and has said repeatedly that its investments will affect financial performance [2][6].

### Segments

PDD reports **one operating and reportable segment**. The chief operating decision makers are the co-CEOs, and no segment profit is disclosed [1]. It does not report Pinduoduo and Temu separately, does not split revenue by country, and states that "substantially all" revenue comes from within China, because the paying customers are Chinese merchants [1]. The only revenue split it gives is by type of service:

*Sources: [1][12]. Growth rates computed from the reported figures.*

**Online marketing services and others** is mainly advertising sold to merchants on Pinduoduo: payment for product listings to appear higher in search results and in the recommendation feed. The 20-F says this revenue is "primarily recognized at a point in time when consumers view or click on the merchants' product listings", or over the advertising period for time-based placements [1]. The "others" component is not broken out. This line is the domestic profit engine, though PDD does not disclose its margin. It grew 29% in FY2024 and 10% in FY2025, then slowed to 2–3% in the first half of 2026 before recovering to 12% in Q3 2026 [12][1][9][8][2].

**Transaction services** consists of commissions and transaction-linked fees charged to merchants on both platforms. It grew 108% in FY2024, from RMB 94.1bn to RMB 195.9bn [12]. Domestic commissions cannot explain a jump of that size, and the timing matches Temu's growth. On the agent basis the 20-F describes, Temu's revenue is the gap between what consumers pay and what merchants receive, and that gap is booked here [1]. The 20-F breaks the FY2025 growth of this line into more active merchants (16.8m, from 15.8m) and higher average revenue per active merchant (RMB 12,742, from RMB 12,399) [1]. Multiplying the two gives the line's total almost exactly. Transaction services fell 12% in Q3 2026, the first decline in the line's history, while online marketing grew 12% [2].

**Realignments.** There have been no segment realignments. Two changes affect comparisons over time. First, the VIE's share of consolidated revenue fell from 45.7% in FY2023 to 13.1% in FY2025, as entities outside the VIE (including Temu's operating companies) grew faster [1]. Second, Temu's mix has been moving from fully managed toward semi-managed and local-seller models (see below). PDD has not disclosed how that shift changes the revenue it books per order.

### How the Business Model Works

#### 1. Where revenue comes from, mechanically

**Pinduoduo (domestic).** Consumers buy goods from third-party merchants on the Pinduoduo app. The original "team purchase" mechanic offered a lower price to buyers who recruited friends to buy the same item, which made the app spread through social sharing [1]. Today the app works mainly as a recommendation feed of deals, with search alongside. Merchants pay PDD in two ways:

- **Advertising (online marketing services).** Merchants bid for traffic: the price of a click or impression is set by auction, and over time PDD has moved merchants toward tools where the merchant sets a target return on ad spend and the platform's algorithm buys traffic to meet it. Revenue is recognised when the consumer views or clicks the listing [1]. For a given level of merchant sales, ad revenue rises with the share of sales merchants spend on ads (the advertising "take rate"). PDD does not disclose that take rate.
- **Transaction-linked fees (transaction services).** These include fees charged on completed orders, such as payment-processing-related technical service fees. Revenue is recognised when the service obligation is complete, at a point in time [1]. Domestic commission rates are low by international standards, and part of the 2024–25 merchant-support programmes consisted of waiving or refunding these fees [10].

Because PDD "does not control the products provided by merchants at any point in time during the transactions," it reports transaction revenue net, as an agent [1]. Coupons and credits given to consumers are recorded either as a reduction of revenue (where they are, in effect, a payment on behalf of merchants) or as sales and marketing expense [1]. That split matters when reading the revenue lines. A subsidy recorded against revenue depresses reported growth, while the same money recorded as marketing does not.

**Temu (global).** Temu runs three main operating models. The secondary estimates of their size come from industry research [11].

- **Fully managed (全托管).** The merchant sets a supply price and ships stock to a Temu-designated warehouse in China. Temu sets the consumer price, runs the marketing, and arranges cross-border shipping, delivery and returns. In 2025 this model made up an estimated 65–70% of Temu's business [11].
- **Semi-managed (半托管).** The merchant holds stock in the destination country and handles fulfilment; Temu runs the storefront and traffic. This model made up an estimated 20–30% of the business in 2025, rising to about 75% in the United States after the de minimis exemption ended [11][13].
- **Local-to-local.** Merchants based in the destination country sell on Temu. It is live in more than 30 countries, and a European version launched in November 2025 [11].

Under all three models PDD reports revenue as an agent. In the fully managed model, the revenue PDD books is the difference between the consumer price and the merchant's supply price, and the cost of moving the goods sits in cost of revenue as fulfilment fees [1][6]. In the semi-managed and local models, PDD does not carry the shipping cost and collects a commission-like fee. Revenue per order is lower there, but so are costs. This is why a shift in Temu's model mix can reduce both reported revenue and cost of revenue without much change in profit. Q3 2026 showed that pattern: transaction services fell RMB 6.4bn while cost of revenue fell RMB 1.6bn and sales and marketing fell RMB 5.0bn [2]. PDD has not attributed these moves to Temu's model mix; the link is an inference.

**First-party brands.** In March 2026 PDD set up a dedicated company with an initial RMB 15bn cash injection. Management has described a plan for about RMB 100bn of investment over the next several years to build first-party brands with manufacturers [4]. Press reports name the company Xinpinmu (新拼姆) and place it in Shanghai [5]. In this model the platform takes on "more product, sales, and inventory risk," giving manufacturers certainty of sales volume so they can invest in research and branding [4]. Press reports say the target categories are apparel, home goods and outdoor products, sold through both Pinduoduo and Temu [5]. On the Q2 2026 call, management said progress had been slower than planned, that third-party listing would remain open, and that first-party and third-party products would "serve different needs" [7]. The company has not said how first-party sales will be recognised or reported. Goods PDD owns would normally be booked gross, so the revenue line could grow faster than the economics once this business scales. That depends on accounting choices the company has not described.

Four features define the economics:

1. **Cost of revenue is mostly Temu fulfilment, plus bandwidth and payment costs.** The company attributes the growth in cost of revenue to "fulfillment fees, bandwidth and server costs, and payment processing fees" [1]. Cost of revenue rose from 37.0% of revenue in FY2023 to 43.7% in FY2025 as Temu grew. A domestic marketplace with no logistics of its own has few direct costs beyond payment and computing, so most of the rise is very likely Temu's shipping (inference).
2. **Sales and marketing is the swing cost.** It includes advertising on outside media, which is the main way Temu buys overseas users, along with TV commercials and the coupons and credits not booked against revenue [1]. In FY2025 the increase in advertising, promotion and coupon spending was RMB 13.0bn [1]. Quarterly sales and marketing has moved from 23% to 35% of revenue over the last eight quarters, because the company raises and cuts spending quickly (see Part 4).
3. **Overheads are tiny.** R&D (3.8%) and G&A (1.9%) together cost less than 6% of revenue. Employees numbered 25,474, of whom 11,389 work in sales, marketing and fulfilment and 10,351 in product development [1]. Headcount grew 46% between end-2023 and end-2025 while revenue grew 74% [1].
4. **Scale works on both sides.** Once the platform exists, extra domestic advertising revenue carries almost no extra cost, which is why online marketing growth drives profit. Temu's model is the opposite: each fully managed order carries its own shipping cost, and each new market needs paid customer acquisition.

**Segment unit economics are not disclosed.** The nearest proxy in the filings is the tax note. In FY2025 PDD earned pre-tax income of RMB 125.7bn in the PRC and recorded a pre-tax *loss* of RMB 6.1bn outside the PRC [1]. The non-PRC figure mixes Temu's overseas operating entities with offshore investment income and holding-company costs, so it is not Temu's profit or loss. It does show that, in aggregate, the overseas entities did not earn a profit in FY2025, and that almost all group profit is taxed in China. Secondary reports suggest Temu cut planned 2025 overseas advertising from about US$4.2bn to about US$1.8bn actually spent, and halted US advertising entirely in May and June 2025 [11].

#### 3. The demand engine

Demand on Pinduoduo is driven by everyday spending on household goods, food, apparel and small electronics, with a bias toward price-sensitive buyers. Food grew fastest across China's online retail in 2025, at 14.5%, against 1.9% for apparel [21]. Agriculture has been strategic for PDD since its founding: the company says it "connect[s] millions of farmers to the digital economy," and in August 2021 it launched a "10 Billion Agriculture Initiative" funded from profits [1]. Purchases are frequent, low in value and largely non-discretionary, which makes the domestic business less cyclical than discretionary categories. The main sensitivity is to how much merchants spend on advertising, not to consumer volumes.

Seasonality is modest. Buyer traffic and orders are lower in the first quarter, and sales are highest in the fourth quarter, which includes the "Double 11" shopping festival [1]. In FY2025 Q1 revenue was 22% of the year's total and Q4 was 29% [1][6].

Temu's demand depends on the price gap it can offer overseas consumers against local retail. That gap depended heavily on duty-free treatment of low-value parcels. The US removed it for China in May 2025 and for all countries in August 2025 [13]. The EU applied a temporary €3 duty per product category to parcels under €150 from 1 July 2026 [14]. Temu's demand engine is therefore increasingly a function of local warehousing and local sellers, not cross-border price arbitrage.

#### 4. The reinforcing structure

The domestic business runs on a supply-side loop. Low commissions and a large volume of price-focused buyers attract factories and farmers who sell direct, without brand or distributor margins. The 20-F says "merchants are drawn by the scale of the sales volume" [1]. Their low prices bring in more buyers, and the platform's algorithm directs traffic toward the products with the best price for the quality. Merchants then compete for that traffic through advertising, which is how PDD earns revenue. The loop runs on prices, not on logistics or content, so it depends on PDD continuing to supply cheap traffic to merchants and on merchants accepting thin margins.

Temu exported the same supply base. Chinese factories already selling on Pinduoduo could reach overseas buyers through Temu's fully managed service without building export capabilities. Management now argues that the next step is to move further up the supply chain: "return to the roots of e-commerce," meaning supply-chain capability [4]. In practice that means first-party brands, merchant upgrading programmes in industrial clusters, and shared overseas warehousing offered to merchants as a service [4][3].

The loop has a known weak point. Consumer switching costs are low, and management itself said on the Q3 2026 call that competition is intense because consumers "easily switch platforms" and "no platform is pulling back" [3]. The loop's defensibility rests on the supply base and the cost structure, not on consumer lock-in.

#### 5. Transaction model transitions under way

Three transitions affect the reported numbers:

- **Temu: cross-border to local.** After the US de minimis exemption ended in May 2025, Temu shifted US sales to semi-managed and local models; secondary estimates put the semi-managed share at about 75% in the US [11]. In Europe the same shift is under way after the 1 July 2026 duty. On the Q2 2026 call Lei Chen said the EU duty means "lower efficiency, higher costs and slower growth" for affected cross-border orders, and that PDD is responding by adding local merchants, building local warehouses and expanding local shipping [7]. Fewer fully managed orders mean less transaction revenue and less fulfilment cost per order.
- **Domestic: fee reductions.** In August 2024 PDD launched a "百亿减免" (roughly RMB 10bn) fee-reduction programme. It waived technical service fees, refunded promotion fees, lowered store deposits and subsidised logistics to remote western regions [10]. In April 2025 it announced a "千亿扶持" (more than RMB 100bn) programme of funds, traffic and other resources over three years [10]. Some of these measures reduce revenue directly; others show up as marketing expense.
- **First-party brands** (see above), not yet visible in the accounts.

#### 7. Funding and capital model

PDD needs almost no capital to grow, and its customers fund it. Buyers pay when they order, and merchants are paid only after delivery and confirmation. The gap shows up as RMB 112.0bn of "payable to merchants" and RMB 18.9bn of merchant deposits at 30 September 2026 [2]. Part of the buyers' cash sits in "restricted cash", RMB 82.2bn, "reserved in a bank supervised account for payments to merchants" [1][2]. Capital expenditure was RMB 1.1bn in FY2025, 0.3% of revenue [1]. The company has no borrowings after repaying its last convertible notes in 2025 [1].

The result is a large and growing cash and investment balance: RMB 455.4bn of cash and short-term investments at 30 September 2026, excluding restricted cash and a further RMB 115.7bn of "other non-current assets" that consist mainly of time deposits and debt securities [2]. That balance earns "interest and investment income", RMB 25.6bn in FY2025 or about a fifth of pre-tax profit [1]. This line includes fair-value changes on RMB 66.0bn of equity securities, so it is volatile: RMB 13.5bn in Q2 2026, then RMB 5.4bn in Q3 2026 [1][8][2]. The company pays no dividends, has no stated plan to pay any, and reports no share repurchases [1].

### Corporate History

PDD's history is short, and a handful of decisions shaped the business:

- **2015 — founding.** Pinduoduo was founded in 2015 by Zheng (Colin) Huang, with a founding team that included Lei Chen, Jiazhen Zhao, Junyun Xiao and Zhenwei Zheng [1]. Several founders came from Xinyoudi Studio, a games and software studio they had run since 2011 [1]. Tencent became a strategic shareholder and still holds 13.8%. A 2018 cooperation agreement gave Pinduoduo access to Weixin Pay entry points [1].
- **July 2018 — Nasdaq IPO.** The IPO raised about US$1.7bn net. Follow-on offerings and private placements in 2019–2020 raised a further US$6.9bn of equity, and two convertible-note issues raised US$3.0bn. The last of the notes was repaid in 2025 [1].
- **2020–2021 — leadership handover and Duo Duo Grocery.** Huang handed the CEO role to Lei Chen in July 2020 and the chairmanship in March 2021 [1]. Duo Duo Grocery launched in 2020, and the 10 Billion Agriculture Initiative in August 2021 [1].
- **September 2022 — Temu.** Temu was founded in Boston and launched in North America in September 2022, then in Oceania (March 2023) and Europe (April 2023) [1]. Transaction services revenue roughly doubled in FY2024 as Temu scaled [12]. The group was renamed PDD Holdings and its principal executive office is now in Dublin [1].
- **2024–2025 — shift to merchant support.** The August 2024 fee-reduction programme and the April 2025 "千亿扶持" programme committed resources to merchants at the expense of near-term profit [10]. In August 2025 management said the quarter's profit levels were "not sustainable" [19]. FY2025 net income fell 13% [1].
- **2025 — trade rules change.** The US removed duty-free treatment of low-value parcels for China in May 2025 and for all countries in August 2025 [13]. The European Commission designated Temu a Very Large Online Platform under the Digital Services Act in May 2024. It adopted preliminary findings against Temu in July 2025 and fined it €200m in May 2026 [1][15].
- **December 2025 – April 2026 — regulatory confrontation in China.** On 3 December 2025, a physical altercation broke out at PDD's Shanghai headquarters during an investigation by Shanghai market regulators acting on a referral from the State Administration for Market Regulation (SAMR). PDD then dismissed several government-relations staff [16]. In January 2026 Bloomberg reported a broadened probe covering alleged fake deliveries, tax matters and other compliance issues [17]. In April 2026 SAMR fined seven platform operators about RMB 3.6bn in total, including about RMB 1.5bn against the Pinduoduo operator, for food-safety and E-Commerce Law violations [1]. In December 2025 Lei Chen and Jiazhen Zhao became co-chairmen as well as co-CEOs [1].
- **March 2026 — first-party brands.** PDD set up the first-party brand company with RMB 15bn of initial capital [4].

No acquisitions, divestitures or failed deals have shaped the company.

# 2. Company Logistics

## 2.1 Customers and Distribution

### Who Are the Customers

PDD has two customer groups, and only one of them pays it directly. **Merchants** pay all of the company's reported revenue, through advertising and transaction fees. **Consumers** buy the goods, but their payments pass through PDD to the merchants. PDD's revenue is only the share it keeps [1]. Analysis of "customers" therefore has to cover both groups.

**Merchants (paying customers).** The platforms had 16.8 million active merchants in FY2025, up from 15.8 million in FY2024. Average transaction-services revenue per active merchant was RMB 12,742 [1]. The merchant base is extremely fragmented, and the 20-F identifies no customer that accounts for 10% or more of revenue [1]. The merchants fall into four broad groups. The company does not report their relative sizes; the groups below are drawn from its own descriptions of its programmes:

| Merchant group | Who they are | How they use PDD | Evidence of scale or activity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Factories and industrial-cluster sellers | Small and mid-sized manufacturers selling direct, often unbranded | Pinduoduo marketplace; Temu fully managed supply; "new quality supply" upgrading programme | Teams visited clusters in Jinhua (stationery), Linyi (auto parts), Shenzhen (eyewear) and Gaoyang (textiles); management says over 60% of Gaoyang textile firms have digitised [3] |
| Farmers, cooperatives and agricultural distributors | Producers of fresh and regional food | Pinduoduo, Duo Duo Grocery, harvest-season programmes | 2026 harvest programme cited at 300,000 merchants across more than 1,000 regions [3] |
| Brand owners and authorised resellers | Branded electronics, appliances, beauty | 百亿补贴 subsidy channel; flagship stores | Not quantified [22] |
| Overseas local sellers | Merchants based in Temu's destination markets | Temu local-to-local and semi-managed models | Local-to-local live in more than 30 countries; European version launched November 2025 (secondary) [11] |

PDD's relationship with merchants has changed since 2024. Fee reductions, store-deposit cuts and logistics subsidies were introduced in August 2024 and widened in April 2025 [10]. The platform's governance rules have also been tightened, with more than 180 measures introduced between January and September 2026 [3]. Merchants carry compliance obligations under their service agreements and must compensate buyers when they break the rules. The company notes that a merchant's compliance record "may affect its sales volume", which means PDD can reduce the traffic a merchant receives [1].

### Distribution Channels

PDD sells only online, through its own apps and websites; it has no physical stores. "Distribution" for PDD means three things: how it acquires consumer traffic, how payment is collected, and how goods reach buyers.

**Traffic acquisition.**

- *Word of mouth and social sharing.* The 20-F says that "direct buyer traffic to our platforms is generated from word-of-mouth referrals by existing buyers" [1]. Pinduoduo's team-purchase mechanic was built for sharing on Weixin (WeChat). The company buys payment processing, advertising and cloud services from Tencent, a related party, worth RMB 6.5bn in FY2025, down from RMB 7.2bn in FY2023 [1].
- *Paid advertising.* PDD says it "continue[s] to invest significantly in marketing activities," including online advertising and television commercials, and offers coupons and credits [1]. For Temu, paid advertising on outside platforms is the main way to acquire users. Secondary estimates put Temu's Q4 2025 overseas ad spend at about US$359m on Meta, US$32.5m on TikTok, US$22.8m on AppLovin and US$20m on Google. For 2025 as a whole, an estimated US$1.83bn was spent against an initial budget of US$4.2bn [11]. These estimates are unaudited and come from industry research.
- *Platform-funded subsidies.* Pinduoduo's 百亿补贴 channel and coupons draw traffic to price-subsidised branded goods. On Double 11 in 2026, Pinduoduo kept its focus on direct price cuts, without the multi-step promotion mechanics other platforms used [22].

**Payment.** The platforms accept credit and debit cards, mobile wallets and buy-now-pay-later options [1]. On Pinduoduo, part of buyers' cash is held in a bank-supervised account until merchants are paid. This "restricted cash" stood at RMB 82.2bn at 30 September 2026 [2][1]. Payment processing fees were RMB 14.3bn in FY2025, or 3.3% of revenue [1].

On the Q3 2026 call Lei Chen described shared warehousing and overseas warehouse operations offered "as services". These let merchants hold stock closer to buyers without large upfront capital [3]. Both shifts move cost and inventory risk around the system. Domestically, PDD absorbs more delivery cost. Overseas, merchants carry more inventory, and Temu's cost per order falls.

- *United States.* Duty-free treatment of parcels under US$800 was removed for goods from China, Hong Kong and Macau with effect from 2 May 2025. It was removed for all countries from 29 August 2025, with specific per-item duties of US$80–200 available for postal shipments until 28 February 2026 [13].
- *European Union.* In 2024, 4.6 billion e-commerce parcels valued under €150 entered the EU, about 91% of them from China. A separate handling fee was agreed on 26 March 2026 [14].

**Pass-through behaviour.** When duties rise, Temu has passed the cost on through prices and reduced orders, then moved to bulk imports by merchants into local warehouses. After the EU duty, ECDB estimates Temu's average order value in the EU rose 37% while its share of EU online transactions halved [25]. In the UK and US, where Temu already ships from local warehouses, ECDB found Temu's average order value stable [25]. Under the local and semi-managed models, merchants pay import duties on bulk shipments, so the cost sits with merchants, not with Temu. PDD does not disclose any figure for duties borne by Temu itself.

# 3. Industry Overview

## 3.1 Industry Overview

### Introduction

PDD competes in two related industries. The first is **Chinese online retail**: platforms that connect China's manufacturers, brands, farmers and retailers with China's consumers. The second is **cross-border discount e-commerce**: platforms that sell goods made in China directly to consumers in other countries, cutting out importers, wholesalers and local retailers.

Online retail exists in China at a scale unmatched elsewhere because three conditions came together. China has a vast, fragmented manufacturing base that had no efficient route to consumers. It built a cheap, dense parcel-delivery system run by private express couriers. And mobile payments became near-universal. Platforms solved the matching problem between millions of small sellers and hundreds of millions of buyers. They make money mainly by selling those sellers visibility (advertising) and by taking a small share of transactions, not by owning the goods. A minority of players, notably JD.com, act as retailers that buy and resell goods, earning a gross margin instead of a fee.

Cross-border discount e-commerce grew out of the domestic industry. From around 2022, platforms including Shein, Temu, AliExpress and TikTok Shop used Chinese supply and, until 2025–26, duty-free treatment of low-value parcels in the US and EU. With these they sold goods abroad at prices local retailers could not match. That price advantage is now being reduced by changes in trade rules, which are pushing the industry toward local warehouses and local sellers [13][14].

### How the Industry Works

**The value chain.** In traditional Chinese retail, goods passed from manufacturer to brand owner, to national and regional distributors, to retailers and then to consumers, with a margin taken at each step. E-commerce platforms removed several steps. A factory selling on Pinduoduo, or a farm selling regional produce, can reach consumers directly. Parcels move through private express companies, most of them franchise networks, and payment runs through mobile wallets that hold funds until delivery is confirmed.

**Business models and their margins.** Four models coexist, and each has a different margin profile:

| Model | Main players | How it earns | Margin profile |
|---|---|---|---|
| Third-party (3P) marketplace | Taobao/Tmall (Alibaba), Pinduoduo | Advertising sold to merchants; commissions and fees | Very high incremental margins; little capital; revenue is a small fraction of GMV |
| First-party (1P) retail | JD.com (core), parts of Tmall | Buys goods and resells; gross margin; logistics fees | Low margins (JD Retail operating margin 5.8% in Q3 2026 [29]); inventory and logistics assets |
| Content and live-stream commerce | Douyin (ByteDance), Kuaishou | Commissions and advertising tied to short video and live streams | Advertising-heavy; integrated with entertainment platforms |
| Instant retail / quick commerce | Meituan, Alibaba (Taobao Instant Commerce), JD | Delivery within about 30 minutes from local stores and warehouses; fees and subsidies | Rider and subsidy costs; heavy losses during the 2025 subsidy war [32] |

**Pricing to merchants.** Marketplaces charge merchants in two ways. Commissions and payment-related fees are low and visible. Advertising is paid through auctions for search and recommendation placement, and is the larger and more profitable component. Total merchant spend as a share of GMV is the platform's "monetisation rate" or "take rate". Platforms raise it mainly by selling more advertising and by moving merchants onto automated bidding tools. Regulators are now limiting how fees can be introduced or changed [27].

**Platform effects.** More buyers attract more sellers, and more sellers mean more choice and lower prices, which attracts more buyers. These effects are real but weaker than in social networks, because both sides use several platforms at once. A merchant can list on Taobao, Pinduoduo, Douyin and JD at the same time, and a consumer can compare prices across apps in seconds. Platforms therefore compete continuously for merchants' attention and consumers' time.

**Cross-border models.** Cross-border platforms use three operating models. In the *fully managed* model, the platform takes goods into its own warehouse in China and handles pricing, export, delivery and returns. In the *semi-managed* model, the merchant holds stock in the destination country and fulfils orders, while the platform runs the storefront and traffic. In the *local-to-local* model, sellers in the destination country use the platform. Shein differs from the marketplaces: it designs and sources its own-brand apparel through on-demand production. Fully managed parcel shipping depended on duty-free thresholds, and those are being removed [13][14].

### Demand Drivers and Cyclicality

**What demand follows.** Online retail of physical goods tracks household spending on everyday goods, which in China has been weak. Total retail sales grew 3.7% in 2025, and December 2025 alone grew only 0.9% [21]. Online channels keep gaining share, at 26.1% of retail in 2025, but more slowly than before [21]. Inside online retail, food is the fastest-growing category (+14.5% in 2025), while apparel is close to flat (+1.9%) [21]. Platform revenue is more volatile than consumer demand, because it also depends on how much merchants spend on advertising. That in turn depends on merchants' margins and on the subsidies platforms offer.

**Seasonality.** Demand peaks in the fourth quarter, around Double 11 (11 November) and the run-up to Chinese New Year. The first quarter is the weakest [1]. The mid-year "618" festival is the second peak.

### Key Industry Trends and Developments

**1. Content commerce takes share.** Douyin, ByteDance's short-video app, has become a major retail platform by combining entertainment, live-stream selling and a growing "shelf" (search and store) business. ByteDance's head of e-commerce put Douyin's 2024 GMV at about RMB 3.43tn, up 35%. Press reports put 2025 GMV above RMB 4tn, with growth above 30% in the first ten months [23]. Goldman Sachs estimated Douyin's 2025 share at about 24% (secondary) [23]. Douyin competes for the same merchants' advertising budgets as Taobao and Pinduoduo.

**2. The instant-retail subsidy war, 2025–26.** In 2025 Alibaba and JD pushed into instant retail and food delivery against Meituan, funded by heavy consumer subsidies. Meituan reported an adjusted net loss of RMB 4.97bn in Q1 2026, against a profit of RMB 10.9bn a year earlier [32]. JD's "New Businesses" (including food delivery) lost RMB 15.7bn in Q3 2025 and RMB 9.6bn in Q3 2026 [29]. Alibaba's quick-commerce revenue grew 45% in the June 2026 quarter [30]. Pinduoduo stayed out. Management said on the Q2 2026 call that instant retail and Duo Duo Grocery have "limited synergy at this stage" [7]. Discounting has eased since early 2026 under regulatory pressure [32].

**3. Regulation turns against "involution".** Chinese policymakers now treat destructive price competition and platform pressure on merchants as policy problems. The Internet Platform Pricing Conduct Rules restrict fee changes, forced price cuts and automatic price-matching [27]. Reporting at the time the rules took effect said platforms were reviewing pricing agreements and splitting service fees into separate components [28]. For marketplaces this narrows the levers for raising monetisation, and it raises compliance costs.

**4. Monetisation pressure on marketplaces.** Platforms have given money back to merchants. Alibaba's customer management revenue grew 10% in the September 2025 quarter, mainly through a higher take rate [31]. By the June 2026 quarter it grew 1% on a like-for-like basis that excludes a "contra revenue impact" from a merchant development programme [30]. PDD cut and refunded merchant fees from August 2024 [10]. Reported advertising growth across the industry fell to low single digits in early 2026, below growth in GMV.

**5. The end of duty-free cross-border parcels.** The US removed its de minimis exemption in 2025 and the EU introduced a €3 duty in July 2026, with full removal of the €150 threshold planned [13][14]. After the EU change, ECDB estimates the combined share of EU online transactions held by Temu, Shein and AliExpress fell from 23% to 12%. Amazon, Zalando and Vinted gained 9.4 percentage points [25]. The industry's response is localisation: bulk shipping, local warehouses and local sellers [11][7].

**6. Platforms move into supply chains and own brands.** Platforms are reaching back into manufacturing. They run "new quality supply" programmes in industrial clusters, offer shared overseas warehousing, and build platform-owned brands. PDD's first-party brand company, with an initial RMB 15bn of capital, is the largest such commitment announced [4][5]. The model trades the asset-light marketplace for inventory risk, in exchange for control over quality and margin.

**7. AI in shopping and promotion.** Platforms are starting to use AI assistants for discovery and promotion. During Double 11 in 2026, Douyin distributed coupons through ByteDance's Doubao assistant, and JD promoted "AI + services" offerings [22]. Platforms with their own AI ecosystems, such as ByteDance and Alibaba, may gain a traffic channel that pure marketplaces lack.

### Level of Competitive Intensity

Competitive intensity is **high** in Chinese e-commerce, and it is **very high** right now in instant retail and in EU cross-border commerce.

*Why it is high.* Switching costs are low on both sides of the market. Consumers compare prices across apps, and merchants list on several platforms. The leading players are all funded by large, profitable parent businesses, such as advertising at ByteDance and cloud and finance at Alibaba, or by large cash balances (PDD). That lets them sustain losses in contested areas. Growth in the underlying market has slowed to about 5% a year [21], so gains increasingly come from rivals rather than from market growth. PDD management described the situation on the Q3 2026 call: competition is intense because consumers "easily switch platforms," and "no platform is pulling back" [3].

*Where price competition bites.* It bites hardest in standard branded goods such as phones, appliances and beauty, where identical products can be compared directly and platforms subsidise prices. It also bites in instant retail and food delivery, where 2025 subsidies produced losses at Meituan, JD and Alibaba [32][29][30]. It bites less in unbranded and regional goods, where Pinduoduo's direct-from-factory and agricultural supply is harder to replicate, and in services such as JD's logistics, where assets create barriers.

*How it varies with the cycle.* Weak consumer demand intensifies competition, because platforms fight for a static pool of spending. Merchants with thin margins also cut advertising, which pushes platforms to offer fee relief. That is the pattern of 2024–26. Regulators have now stepped in against the most aggressive forms of subsidy and price pressure, which should reduce price wars but also limit how platforms monetise [27][32].

*Barriers to entry versus barriers to scale.* Opening a marketplace is not hard, but reaching scale is. That takes hundreds of millions of users, millions of merchants, payment and logistics integration, and years of losses. Since Pinduoduo, Douyin is the only new entrant to reach Tier 1, because it brought its own audience. In cross-border commerce, barriers are rising as duty-free thresholds disappear and compliance obligations under the EU Digital Services Act grow [14][15].

*Recent evidence.* The recent evidence includes the 2025 food-delivery subsidy war and its losses [32]; Alibaba's like-for-like customer management revenue slowing to +1% [30]; PDD's merchant fee waivers [10]; and Temu's halving of EU transaction share after the July 2026 duty [25].

### Basis of Competition

| Dimension | What it means in this industry | Who it favours |
|---|---|---|
| Price | Lowest price for comparable goods; subsidies; direct-from-factory supply | Pinduoduo, Temu; whoever is willing to subsidise |
| Assortment and supply depth | Breadth of merchants, regional and unbranded goods, brand authorisation | Taobao (breadth), Pinduoduo (factory and farm supply), Tmall and JD (brands) |
| Discovery and content | Ability to show buyers products they did not search for: feeds, short video, live streams | Douyin, Kuaishou; Pinduoduo's recommendation feed |
| Delivery speed and service | Same-day or next-day delivery; returns; instant retail | JD, Meituan, Alibaba (instant commerce) |
| Merchant economics | Total cost of selling (fees, ads, deposits); traffic quality | Platforms offering fee relief; Pinduoduo since 2024 |
| Trust and compliance | Product quality, counterfeit control, food safety, regulatory standing | Tmall, JD; a growing requirement for all, especially Temu in the EU [15] |
| Scale and balance sheet | Ability to fund subsidies and absorb losses | Alibaba, ByteDance, PDD (RMB 455bn of cash and short-term investments [2]) |
| Cross-border cost position | Duty treatment, local warehouses, shipping cost | Amazon and local retailers after 2025–26 rule changes; localised Temu |

### Structural Dynamics and Outlook

*Consolidation.* The Chinese market has consolidated into four large general platforms and several specialists. No acquisitions are expected among them, because antitrust rules and the platforms' own scale stand in the way. Consolidation happens through share shifts, not deals.

*Compounding scale advantages.* Scale gives a platform cheaper customer acquisition, more data for recommendation and ad targeting, and stronger bargaining power with couriers. These advantages favour the existing leaders but do not stop them from losing share to one another, as Douyin's rise shows [23].

*Disruption risks.* The industry faces three disruption risks. The first is content and AI-led discovery, which could move traffic to platforms that own entertainment or AI assistants [23][22]. The second is regulation that caps fees and restricts pricing tools [27]. The third is the closure of trade loopholes for cross-border platforms [13][14].

*Contested territory.* The contested territory has moved in three directions. Instant retail is the main contested area domestically [32]. In cross-border commerce, the EU is the main contested market after the July 2026 duty, and local fulfilment is becoming a requirement there [25][14]. In supply chains, platforms are building or acquiring the ability to design and source products themselves [4].

**Summary.** Chinese online retail is a large, mature market growing at about 5% a year. Four platforms compete intensely for merchants' advertising budgets, and regulators now restrict both fee increases and subsidy wars. Cross-border discount e-commerce is shifting from duty-free parcel shipping to local warehouses and local sellers, which narrows the price advantage that drove its growth.

### Three Things to Understand About the Financials

**1. Audited vs. unaudited FY2025.** The audited 20-F reports FY2025 G&A of RMB 8,157.7m, operating profit of RMB 93,102.1m and net income of RMB 97,842.5m [1]. The four unaudited quarterly releases sum to G&A of RMB 6,636m, operating profit of RMB 94,625m and net income of RMB 99,364.5m [9][8][18][6][2]. The difference, about RMB 1.52bn, sits entirely in G&A. The 20-F also discloses that in April 2026, after the year-end, SAMR fined the Pinduoduo operator "about RMB1.5 billion" [1]. The amounts match, which suggests the fine was booked into FY2025 as an adjusting subsequent event. The company has not said so explicitly; this is an inference. It means the FY2025 non-GAAP figures in the March 2026 release (operating profit RMB 102.6bn, net income RMB 107.3bn, EPS RMB 72.38) are about RMB 1.5bn higher than the audited accounts imply [6].

**2. Net income is a poor guide to the operating trend in 2026.** In the first nine months of 2026, operating profit rose RMB 11.6bn year on year, but net income fell RMB 5.6bn [2]. Three things absorbed the gain: an "other" loss of RMB 9.5bn against a RMB 3.3bn gain a year earlier, lower investment income (RMB 18.3bn against RMB 19.2bn), and RMB 3.5bn more tax [2]. Until the company explains the 2026 "other" loss, operating profit is the cleaner measure of business performance. That statement is about measurement, not about value.

**3. The cash is large, but not all of it is spare.** Cash, cash equivalents and short-term investments were RMB 455.4bn at 30 September 2026, about US$67.9bn at that date's rate. Including restricted cash and the long-term deposits in other non-current assets, the total is about RMB 653bn (computed) [2]. Against this sit RMB 112.0bn owed to merchants and RMB 18.9bn of merchant deposits [2]. At the end of 2025, RMB 125.9bn of net assets in the PRC subsidiaries and VIE were legally restricted from distribution, and 43% of cash, restricted cash and short-term investments were held in mainland China [1]. The company says it plans to "indefinitely reinvest a majority of its undistributed earnings" [1].

### Key Risks to the Outlook

**Trade rules for cross-border parcels.** The EU's €3 duty per product category took effect on 1 July 2026. A handling fee has been agreed, and the €150 threshold is due to be removed entirely around 2028 [14]. In the first quarter under the duty, transaction services fell 12% [2]. Third-party data shows Temu's EU transaction share halving [25]. Management has said affected cross-border orders face "lower efficiency, higher costs and slower growth" [7]. The US exemption is already gone [13]. Further changes, such as the new EU handling fee or similar rules elsewhere, would hit the fully managed model again.

**Regulatory enforcement in China.** In April 2026 SAMR fined the Pinduoduo operator about RMB 1.5bn for food-safety and E-Commerce Law violations, and suspended onboarding of new cake vendors for nine months [1]. This followed the December 2025 altercation with Shanghai regulators [16] and a reported investigation covering alleged fake deliveries and tax matters [17]. The Internet Platform Pricing Conduct Rules, effective April 2026, require public consultation before fee changes and ban forced price cuts and automatic price-matching [27]. Together these limit how PDD can raise merchant fees and raise its compliance costs. Management has introduced more than 180 governance measures in 2026 [3].

**Expiry of the preferential tax rate.** The 15% high-and-new-technology rate for the main operating subsidiaries is available "until 2026" [1]. In FY2025 the preferential rate reduced the effective tax rate by 8.0 percentage points [1]. The 20-F does not say whether renewal has been applied for or granted. If the status lapsed, the main subsidiaries would pay the standard 25% rate [1].

**Open-ended investment programmes.** The 100 Billion Support Program runs to about April 2028, and the first-party brand plan contemplates about RMB 100bn "over the next few years" [10][4]. Management has given no end date for the "strategic investment phase" and no margin target [3][4]. The first-party brand business introduces inventory risk and possible channel conflict with the platform's own merchants. Management says first-party and third-party goods will "serve different needs" [7].

**Domestic competition.** Douyin's GMV grew more than 30% in 2025 [23], and management describes competition as intense, with no platform pulling back [3]. Alibaba and JD have reduced subsidy spending in 2026 [29][30], but further subsidy rounds, including any new push in instant retail, where PDD is not present, could pressure Pinduoduo's traffic and merchants' advertising budgets.

**EU Digital Services Act follow-on.** The Commission fined Temu €200m in May 2026 and set 28 August 2026 as the deadline for Temu's action plan [15]. Fines under the DSA can reach 6% of global annual turnover [1]. In November 2026 the Commission was reported to have found, preliminarily, that Temu falls short on illegal products; a confirmed finding could bring a further fine of up to 6% of global turnover, and Temu disputes it [33]. The outcome depends on the Commission's assessment of Temu's compliance.

### Financial Overview

- *Transaction services* grew 108% in FY2024 with Temu's expansion [12]. It was nearly flat in Q2 2025 (+1%), the quarter in which the US de minimis exemption ended [19][13]. It rebounded to +19–20% in Q4 2025 and Q1 2026 [6][9], and fell 12% in Q3 2026, the first quarter of the EU duty [2][14]. The Q3 2026 release gives no explanation for the decline. On the Q2 2026 call, management said cross-border orders in markets affected by the EU duty "will face lower fulfillment efficiency and higher costs" [7].
- *Sales and marketing* is the main swing factor, ranging from 23% to 35% of revenue across eight quarters. It is seasonally high in Q1, when revenue is lowest, and it moves with Temu's overseas ad buying. It fell from 34.9% in Q1 2025 to 26.2% in Q2 2025, the quarter in which secondary sources report Temu halted US advertising [11]. It fell again to 23.3% in Q3 2026, down 17% year on year to RMB 25.3bn [2]. The Q3 2026 release attributes the opex decline "primarily" to lower sales and marketing, without further explanation [2]. On the Q3 2026 call Jun Liu said expense ratios in any quarter "fluctuate with the pace of business rollouts and their return cycles" and should not be extrapolated [3].

**How consumer incentives are booked.** Coupons and subsidies for consumers fall into one of two lines. If PDD has an explicit or implicit obligation to fund them on merchants' behalf, they reduce revenue; otherwise they are sales and marketing expense [1]. The auditor flags this classification as a critical audit matter, because it turns on management's judgment of "implicit obligations" [1]. So a fall in sales and marketing can reflect less advertising, fewer incentives, or incentives moving into the revenue line. The quarterly releases do not say which [2].
- **Other loss.** Other income turned into a loss of RMB 9.5bn in the first nine months of 2026, including RMB 7.4bn in Q2 2026 alone. The releases give no explanation [2][8]. Q2 2026 included the EU's €200m DSA fine (May 2026) [15], which at most accounts for about RMB 1.6bn. The April 2026 SAMR fine appears to have been accrued in FY2025 (see below). The rest of the Q2 loss is unexplained. The equity portfolio is one candidate, but the company does not name its holdings. At end-2025 the equity securities had a cost of RMB 47.2bn against a fair value of RMB 66.0bn, and fair-value gains were RMB 10.4bn in FY2025 [1].
The two main operating subsidiaries, Shanghai Xunmeng and Walnut Street (Shanghai), pay a 15% rate as high and new technology enterprises, Shanghai Xunmeng since 2018 and Walnut Street since 2021. The 20-F says this rate is available "until 2026" [1]. In FY2025 the preferential rate saved RMB 9.6bn of tax, about 10% of net income (computed) [1]. The effective rate was 16.5% in FY2023, 15.3% in FY2024, 18.2% in FY2025 and about 19.5% in the first nine months of 2026 (computed) [1][2]. The valuation allowance line reflects losses in entities with "cumulative losses and no forecast profits," against which no tax benefit is recognised [1]. These are most likely overseas entities, given the non-PRC pre-tax loss of RMB 6.1bn, but that is an inference. Quarterly rates in 2026 (18–25%) are above 2025's (12–26%) on average.
**3. The cash is large, but not all of it is spare.** Cash, cash equivalents and short-term investments were RMB 455.4bn at 30 September 2026, about US$67.9bn at that date's rate. Including restricted cash and the long-term deposits in other non-current assets, the total is about RMB 653bn (computed) [2]. Against this sit RMB 112.0bn owed to merchants and RMB 18.9bn of merchant deposits [2]. At the end of 2025, RMB 125.9bn of net assets in the PRC subsidiaries and VIE were legally restricted from distribution, and 43% of cash, restricted cash and short-term investments were held in mainland China [1]. The company says it plans to "indefinitely reinvest a majority of its undistributed earnings" [1]. The listed parent held only RMB 46m of cash itself, and the 20-F says it is "possible" PDD was a passive foreign investment company for 2025 (see Company Overview) [1].

The 20-F states that it has no "present plan to pay any cash dividends on our ordinary shares in the foreseeable future." It reports no share repurchases, and Item 16E is marked "Not applicable" [1]. Management's stated priority for cash is investment: supply chains, merchant support, first-party brands and logistics [6][3][4]. The clearest statement came on the Q2 2024 call, when Lei Chen said buybacks and dividends were not appropriate and not needed "in the foreseeable future"; the ADSs fell 28.5% that day [43][44]. **Underlying assumptions management has stated.** None are quantified. Management has said that e-commerce penetration has room to grow in specific categories, especially agriculture [3], and that competition will stay intense, with "no platform is pulling back" [3]. It has also said "short-term volatility will not change the long-term direction of our global business" [7].
**Trade rules for cross-border parcels.** The EU's €3 duty per product category took effect on 1 July 2026. A handling fee has been agreed, and the €150 threshold is due to be removed entirely around 2028 [14]. In the first quarter under the duty, transaction services fell 12% [2]. Third-party data shows Temu's EU transaction share halving [25]. Management has said affected cross-border orders "will face lower fulfillment efficiency and higher costs" [7]. The US exemption is already gone [13]. Further changes, such as the new EU handling fee or similar rules elsewhere, would hit the fully managed model again.
**Regulatory enforcement in China.** In April 2026 SAMR fined the Pinduoduo operator about RMB 1.5bn for food-safety and E-Commerce Law violations, and suspended onboarding of new cake vendors for nine months [1]. The decision (国市监处罚〔2026〕22号) imposed a fine of RMB 1.514bn and confiscated RMB 5.84m, the largest of seven penalties in the "ghost takeaway" (幽灵外卖) case; Meituan, JD and Taobao Shangou were penalised about RMB 0.75bn, 0.64bn and 0.56bn [40][41][42]. PDD was the only platform fined at the top rate of RMB 160,000 per unchecked store. The decision states that during the investigation it repeatedly refused to provide materials, supplied false information and used "violence and soft resistance" to obstruct enforcement [40][41]. Co-CEO Jiazhen Zhao, the legal representative, was fined RMB 6.94m personally [42]. PDD said it accepted the penalty [40]. This followed the December 2025 altercation with Shanghai regulators [16] and a reported investigation covering alleged fake deliveries and tax matters [17]. The Internet Platform Pricing Conduct Rules, effective April 2026, require public consultation before fee changes and ban forced price cuts and automatic price-matching [27]. In its December 2025 – March 2026 self-inspection under those rules, the NDRC says the regulators targeted traffic-algorithm rules that force merchants, openly or in disguise, to cut or follow prices [39]. Together these limit how PDD can raise merchant fees and raise its compliance costs. Management has introduced more than 180 governance measures in 2026 [3].
**Expiry of the preferential tax rate.** The 15% high-and-new-technology rate for the main operating subsidiaries is available "until 2026" [1]. In FY2025 the preferential rate reduced the effective tax rate by 8.0 percentage points, worth RMB 9.6bn or about 10% of net income (computed) [1]. The 20-F does not say whether renewal has been applied for or granted. If the status lapsed, the main subsidiaries would pay the standard 25% rate [1].
**Open-ended investment programmes.** The 100 Billion Support Program runs to about April 2028, and the first-party brand plan contemplates RMB 100bn "over the next 3 years" [10][4]. Management has given no end date for the "strategic investment phase" and no margin target [3][4]. The first-party brand business introduces inventory risk and possible channel conflict with the platform's own merchants. Management says first-party and third-party products "will complement each other" [7].
**EU Digital Services Act follow-on.** The Commission fined Temu €200m on 28 May 2026 because its 2024 risk assessment fell short of the DSA; it found that EU consumers are "very likely to encounter illegal items" on Temu [37]. Temu had until 28 August 2026 to submit an action plan [15]. On 17 November 2026 the Commission issued a second, preliminary finding, this time on risk mitigation (Article 35) [38]. It cited ineffective product-compliance controls, a weak content-moderation system and an ineffective sanction system [38]. Secondary reports add that test reports are required for only a small share of products and often go unchecked, and that sanctions fall on individual stores rather than the traders behind them [56]. A confirmed breach could bring a fine of up to 6% of worldwide annual turnover, and Temu disputes the findings [38].

**US state enforcement.** The 20-F lists consumer-protection and privacy suits by US state attorneys general and a US$2m civil penalty under the INFORM Consumers Act in September 2025 [1]. Texas sued in February 2026 and settled in October 2026. PDD and Whaleco agreed to pay US$3m, store Texas consumers' data on US servers controlled by a US company, limit access from China and meet product-testing obligations, while denying the allegations [53]. Iowa sued in July 2026, and other state suits remain open (secondary) [54].

**Governance and related parties.** The payment licence used alongside PDD's platforms sits in a company controlled by two executives and funded by PDD loans. The founder's votes are exercised by the board, and changes to the board's size, election procedures or the Partnership's rights need 95% of votes cast (see Company Overview) [1][36]. SAMR's finding of obstruction in April 2026 shows that how the company behaves toward regulators can become a cost in itself [40].
